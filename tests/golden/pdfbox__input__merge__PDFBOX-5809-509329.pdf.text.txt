| Endocrine Disruptors and Testis Development\x20
| \x20
| Project Scope\x20
| Declines in human and animal male fertility\x20
| have been observed in recent years by\x20
| several researchers.  Possibly related\x20
| declines also have been observed in sperm\x20
| concentrations and related indicators of\x20
| semen quality.  Rates of testicular cancer and\x20
| congenital abnormalities such as\x20
| cryptorchidism and hypospadias also appear\x20
| to be increasing.  The declines in semen\x20
| quality vary regionally, suggesting an\x20
| association with exposure to endocrine\x20
| disrupting chemicals (EDCs), which can\x20
| influence normal sex determination, testis\x20
| development, and sperm viability.  The\x20
| objective of this grant was to develop a better\x20
| understanding of how endocrine disruptors\x20
| affect testis development and function in\x20
| mammals. \x20
| \x20
| The development of the testis requires the\x20
| coordination of the growth and differentiation\x20
| of several cell types. The testis is formed\x20
| initially from an undifferentiated bipotential\x20
| gonad. The first sign of male development is\x20
| the differentiation of precursor Sertoli cells,\x20
| which then aggregate with germ cells and\x20
| undergo a transition from mesenchyme to\x20
| epithelia to form testicular cords.  Another\x20
| characteristic of the development of the testis\x20
| after the formation of cords is rapid growth\x20
| compared to the analogous ovarian\x20
| precursor. The peak of Sertoli cell\x20
| proliferation occurs just before birth, and cell\x20
| proliferation in the embryonic and early\x20
| postnatal periods is crucial for establishing\x20
| the mature adult size of the testis.  In addition, this proliferation is necessary for reproductive function, in\x20
| order to maintain a sufficient population of sperm-producing Sertoli cells during adulthood. \x20
| Grant Title and Principal Investigator\x20
| Endocrine Disruptors and Testis Development\x20
| Michael K. Skinner – Center for Reproductive Biology,\x20
| Washington State University, Pullman, WA\x20
| EPA STAR Grant #R827405\x20
| \x20
| Key Findings and Implications\x20
| • An embryonic testis organ culture system was\x20
| developed to assess the effects of endocrine disruptors\x20
| on organ morphogenesis and tissue function.  Methods\x20
| also were developed to assess transforming growth\x20
| factor (TGF) and neurotropin (NT) gene expression and\x20
| gene product levels. \x20
| • Endocrine disruptors (e.g., methoxychlor [MXC] and\x20
| vinclozolin) were found to alter early embryonic testis\x20
| development when maternal exposures occurred at the\x20
| time of fetal sex determination and testis morpho-
| genesis. \x20
| • In vivo exposure of gestating rats to MXC\x20
| demonstrated a heritable reduction in sperm function\x20
| and increased spermatogenic cell apoptosis affecting\x20
| males in generations F1 through F4. Studies show that\x20
| this heritable effect may be associated with alteration of\x20
| the DNA methylation patterns in male germ cell\x20
| precursors.\x20
| \x20
| Publications include ten peer reviewed articles.\x20
| \x20
| Project Period: August 1999 to July 2002\x20
| \x20
| Preliminary research indicates that two families of paracrine growth factors directly influence testis\x20
| development and function.  Members of the transforming growth factor family (for example, TGF-")\x20
| appear to be critical for embryonic testis growth, and at least one neurotropin (neurotropin 3 or NT3)\x20
| appears to have a critical role in the morphogenesis of testis development.  Abnormal testis development\x20
| and male infertility caused by endocrine disruptors may, in part, be mediated through pathways involving\x20
| these factors. \x20
| \x20
| Thus, the hypothess initially tested under this grant was that endocrine distruptors affect locally produced\x20
| paracrine growth factors that are essential for testicular cell growth and differentiation during embryonic\x20
| and postnatal development.  The adverse developmental effects can later manifest themselves as\x20
| reduced male fertility and sperm production.  Experiments investigated the effects of endocrine disruptors\x20
| on TGFs  and neurotropins during testis development effects on male fertility.  Environmental agents with\x20
| estrogenic activity (i.e., methoxychlor [MXC], a general use organochlorine pesticide) and antiandrogenic\x20
| activity (i.e., vinclozoline, a selective contact fungicide) were assessed using a rat embryonic testis organ\x20
| culture system and a rat in vivo model.  \x20

| Project Results and Implications\x20
|   \x20
| Treatment of gestating mothers had no major effect on fetal sex determination or gross testis histology at\x20
| any time during development.  However, animals exposed in utero between embryonic day 8 (E8) and\x20
| E15 experienced pubertal and adult spermatogenic defects in the F1 generation. Treated animals also\x20
| had an increase in spermatogenic cell apoptosis (programmed cell death).  Similar results were observed\x20
| at postnatal day 20 (P20) or P60 for MCX and vinclozoline.  In addition to this decreased spermatogenic\x20
| cell survival, sperm motility was impaired and morphology was abnormal.  Interestingly, animals exposed\x20
| in utero to the same dose of endocrine disruptor later in development (E25-E20) had no spermatogenic\x20
| cell defects. This finding is consistent with the occurrence of critical processes such as germ cell DNA\x20
| remethylation, cord formation, and sex determination during the E10-E15 period, which are complete after\x20
| E15. \x20
| \x20
| These studies has provided insight into the mechanisms by which endocrine disruptors influence testis\x20
| growth and function.  One key observation was the transgenerational effects of a transient in utero\x20
| exposure to the endocrine disruptors MXC and vinclozolin on testis function and gametogenesis. In\x20
| particular, these studies demonstrated the ability of MXC and vinclozolin to promote a transgenerational\x20
| phenotype in spermatogenic cells\x20
| \x20
| Additional experiments were conducted that demonstrated the effects of exposure of endocrine disruptors\x20
| on the F1 offspring could be passed to subsequent generations.  When the male rats of the F2 generation\x20
| (derived from the animals discussed above) were examined, the same increased apoptosis rate in\x20
| spermatogenesc cells was identified as the F1 generation.  Specifically, a three- to five-fold increase in\x20
| apoptosis was observed in F2 rats at maturity.  Also, an approximately 20 percent decrease in sperm\x20
| motility and forward movement also was observed.  These experiments show that EDC exposures to a\x20
| gestating dam at the critical period of fetal sex determination and testis morphogenesis (i.e., cord\x20
| formation) appears to cause a germline effect, i.e., a decrease in spermatogenic capacity and sperm\x20
| viability that seems to be heritable in the male.  This experiment was repeated with four different gestating\x20
| mothers, and similar results were observed through the F4 generation.  An outcross of an affected treated\x20
| male with a wild type untreated female slightly reduced the magnitude of the effect, but the basic feature\x20
| of the phenotype (i.e., increased spermatogenic apoptosis) remained.\x20
| \x20
| Researchers postulated that the transgenerational effect of maternal EDC exposures male\x20
| spermatogenesis arises through disruption of DNA methylation in the male germline.  This hypothesis\x20
| was investigated by exposing gestating dams to 100 mg/kg MXC from E8-E15, and collecting testes for\x20
| analysis on P6.  DNA was isolated and methylation state determined with methylation-sensitive restriction\x20
| enzymes and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) methods.  A number of PCR products with altered\x20
| methylation patterns were detected in MXC-treated cells.  This observation further supports the proposal\x20
| that the epigenetic effects of MXC on spermatogenesis are mediated by changes in DNA methylation.  \x20
| \x20
| \x20
| \x20
| Relevance to ORD’s Multi-Year Research Plan\x20
| This project contributes to all three of ORD’s Multi-Year Plan long-term goals (LTGs 1-3), in particular\x20
| (1) providing a better understanding of the science underlying the effects and assessment of\x20
| endocrine disruptors, (2) determining the extent of the impact of endocrine disruptors on humans and\x20
| wildlife, and (3) supporting EPA’s screening and testing program.  More specifically, the results\x20
| address the effects of early developmental exposure to endocrine disrupting pesticides on\x20
| reproductive function in adults, toxicant-induced alterations in mammalian reproductive development,\x20
| dose response relationships, critical periods of exposure, in vivo tissue levels of the active toxicant, in\x20
| vivo and in vitro mechanisms of action, androgenic and anti-androgenic activity, the effects of\x20
| endocrine disruptors on growth factors and receptors that control spermatogenesis and growth and\x20
| development of the testis, and in vitro and in vivo  assessment methods.\x20

| Treatment of gestating mothers had no major effect on fetal sex determination or gross testis histology at <EOL>

| These studies has provided insight into the mechanisms by which endocrine disruptors influence testis <EOL>
| growth and function.  One key observation was the transgenerational effects of a transient in utero <EOL>
| exposure to the endocrine disruptors MXC and vinclozolin on testis function and gametogenesis. In <EOL>
| particular, these studies demonstrated the ability of MXC and vinclozolin to promote a transgenerational <EOL>
| phenotype in spermatogenic cells <EOL>

| Additional experiments were conducted that demonstrated the effects of exposure of endocrine disruptors <EOL>
| on the F1 offspring could be passed to subsequent generations.  When the male rats of the F2 generation <EOL>
| (derived from the animals discussed above) were examined, the same increased apoptosis rate in <EOL>
| spermatogenesc cells was identified as the F1 generation.  Specifically, a three- to five-fold increase in <EOL>
| apoptosis was observed in F2 rats at maturity.  Also, an approximately 20 percent decrease in sperm <EOL>
| motility and forward movement also was observed.  These experiments show that EDC exposures to a <EOL>
| gestating dam at the critical period of fetal sex determination and testis morphogenesis (i.e., cord <EOL>
| formation) appears to cause a germline effect, i.e., a decrease in spermatogenic capacity and sperm <EOL>
| viability that seems to be heritable in the male.  This experiment was repeated with four different gestating <EOL>
| mothers, and similar results were observed through the F4 generation.  An outcross of an affected treated <EOL>
| male with a wild type untreated female slightly reduced the magnitude of the effect, but the basic feature <EOL>
| of the phenotype (i.e., increased spermatogenic apoptosis) remained. <EOL>
|  <EOL>
| Researchers postulated that the transgenerational effect of maternal EDC exposures male <EOL>
| spermatogenesis arises through disruption of DNA methylation in the male germline.  This hypothesis <EOL>
| was investigated by exposing gestating dams to 100 mg/kg MXC from E8-E15, and collecting testes for <EOL>
| analysis on P6.  DNA was isolated and methylation state determined with methylation-sensitive restriction <EOL>

| methylation patterns were detected in MXC-treated cells.  This observation further supports the proposal <EOL>
| that the epigenetic effects of MXC on spermatogenesis are mediated by changes in DNA methylation.   <EOL>
