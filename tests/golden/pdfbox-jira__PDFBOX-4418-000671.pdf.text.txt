| Received 6 June 2007; received in revised form 4 September 2007; accepted 5 September 2007 Abstract Bark beetle-caused tree mortality in conifer forests affects the quantity and quality of forest fuels and has long been assumed to increase fire\x20
| hazard and potential fire behavior. In reality, bark beetles, and their effects on fuel accumulation, and subsequent fire hazard, are poorly understood.\x20
| We extensively sampled fuels in three bark beetle-affected Intermountain conifer forests and compared these data to existing research on bark\x20
| beetle/fuels/fire interactions within the context of the disturbance regime. Data were collected in endemic, epidemic and post-epidemic stands of\x20
| Douglas-fir, lodgepole pine and Engelmann spruce. From these data, we evaluated the influence of bark beetle-caused tree mortality on various\x20
| fuels characteristics over the course of a bark beetle rotation. The data showed that changes in fuels over time create periods where the potential for\x20
| high intensity and/or severe fires increases or decreases. The net result of bark beetle epidemics was a substantial change in species composition\x20
| and a highly altered fuels complex. Early in epidemics there is a net increase in the amount of fine surface fuels when compared to endemic stands.\x20
| In post-epidemic stands large, dead, woody fuels, and live surface fuels dominate. We then discuss potential fire behavior in bark beetle-affected\x20
| conifer fuels based on actual and simulated fuels data. Results indicated that for surface fires both rates of fire spread and fireline intensities were\x20
| higher in the current epidemic stands than in the endemic stands. Rates of spread and fireline intensities were higher in epidemic stands due,\x20
| however, to decreased vegetative sheltering and its effect on mid-flame wind speed, rather than changes in fuels. Passive crown fires were more\x20
| likely in post-epidemic stands, but active crown fires were less likely due to decreased aerial fuel continuity. We also discuss the ecological effects\x20
| of extreme fire behavior. Information is presented on managing forests to reduce the impact of bark beetle outbreaks and the interplay between\x20
| management, bark beetle populations, fuels and fire hazard and behavior.\x20

| disturbance in Intermountain Forests over varying temporal and spatial scales. 1. Introduction\x20
| The interactions among disturbances operating over various\x20
| spatial and temporal scales determine the nature of forested\x20
| landscapes. In some western forest systems, for example fire-
| dependent lodgepole pine forests, the influence of a single\x20
| disturbance agent, such as wildfire, is so pervasive that it\x20
| dominates forest dynamics. In most low to mid-elevation\x20
| ponderosa pine forests, the relatively rapid accumulation of\x20
| fuels and weather conditions required for fire ignition and\x20
| spread make seasonal fires common. Fire return intervals from\x20
| as few as 5 up to 100 or more years are capable of driving forest\x20
| succession (Jenkins et al., 1998). In high elevation, subalpine\x20
| forests, lightning is relatively common and sufficient amounts\x20
| of fuels accumulate to sustain fire. However, short snow-free\x20
| periods, low temperatures, and high relative humidities often\x20
| preclude fire occurrence for extended periods (Arno, 2000). In\x20
| these forest systems, Dendroctonus bark beetles (Coleoptera:\x20
| Curculionidae, Scolytinae) coupled with other widespread\x20
| disturbance events such as harvesting, snow avalanches and\x20
| wind throw primarily regulate forest dynamics (Veblen et al.,\x20
| 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998). A conceptual model of the\x20
| interactions of agents of disturbance over large spatial and long\x20
| temporal scales is presented in Fig. 1.\x20
| Bark beetles naturally occur throughout the ranges of their\x20
| host trees and have a critical role within healthy, functioning\x20
| ecosystems (Roe and Amman, 1970; Geiszler et al., 1980; Gara\x20
| et al., 1985; Veblen et al., 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998; Bebi et al.,\x20
| 2003; Kulakowski et al., 2003). During endemic population\x20
| phases, bark beetles typically infest blowdown and trees\x20
| weakened by fire, diseases, and drought (Schmid, 1981; Raffa\x20
| and Berryman, 1983; Mattson and Haack, 1987; Goheen and\x20
| Hansen, 1993; Cunningham et al., 2005). Periodically, popula­
| tions of bark beetles reach outbreak levels infesting otherwise\x20
| healthy trees (Raffa, 2001; Samman and Logan, 2000).\x20
| The level of bark beetle activity fluctuates with the\x20
| availability of suitable host material, stand conditions,\x20
| environmental factors, and the abundance of parasites and\x20
| predators. Although many factors may contribute to the\x20
| initiation of outbreaks, stand conditions largely determine the potential for bark beetle population growth and spread\x20
| (McCambridge and Knight, 1972; Schmid and Frye, 1977;\x20
| Cole and Amman, 1980; Bartos and Amman, 1989; Anhold\x20
| et al., 1996; Hansen et al., 2001; Hindmarch and Reid, 2001;\x20
| Fettig et al., 2007; Hebertson and Jenkins, 2007). Historically,\x20
| forest dynamics and patterns resulting from various fire regimes\x20
| largely influenced the susceptibility of stands to bark beetle\x20
text: default page 3
| 18 M.J. Jenkins et al. / Forest Ecology and Management 254 (2008) 16–34 infestation. More recently, management practices including fire\x20
| suppression and exclusion, livestock grazing, and timber\x20
| harvesting have altered forest structure and composition (Arno,\x20
| 1980; Covington et al., 1997). The specific effect of altered\x20
| forest composition and structure on bark beetle epidemiology\x20
| will vary with tree species, the effectiveness of fire suppression,\x20
| historic fire regime and geographic parameters. One conse­
| quence of change in some forest types is that larger and more\x20
| contiguous landscapes have become simultaneously suscep­
| tible to bark beetle outbreaks due to the high percentage of old,\x20
| large diameter, and less vigorous host trees (Covington et al.,\x20
| 1997; Samman and Logan, 2000; Gallant et al., 2003). In\x20
| general, factors that increase susceptible tree numbers and\x20
| decrease heterogeneity increase bark beetle hazard (Amman,\x20
| 1977; Schmid and Frye, 1976; Rudinsky, 1966). After\x20
| reviewing extensive literature, Keane et al. (2002) concluded\x20
| that fire suppression programs have resulted in atypical fuel\x20
| accumulations, declines in forest health, greater risk to\x20
| firefighters and increased likelihood of insect and disease\x20
| epidemics. Sibold and others (2006), however, found little\x20
| evidence to support the assertion that fire suppression practices\x20
| have altered the historic range of variability in their study area\x20
| in Rocky Mountain National Park.\x20
| In the Intermountain West, Douglas-fir beetle (D. pseudot­
| sugae Hopkins), mountain pine beetle (D. ponderosae\x20
| Hopkins), and spruce beetle (D. rufipennis Kirby) are capable\x20
| of causing extensive mortality of host trees in relatively short\x20
| periods of time (Mielke, 1950; Massey and Wygant, 1954;\x20
| Amman, 1977; Schmid and Frye, 1977; Furniss and Orr, 1978;\x20
| McGregor and Cole, 1985; Amman et al., 1988b; Patterson,\x20
| 1991; Schmitz and Gibson, 1996; Holsten et al., 1999; Samman\x20
| and Logan, 2000; Dymerski et al., 2001). This mortality alters\x20
| the amount, composition and arrangement of living and dead\x20
| biomass in various fuel complexes (Schmid and Frye, 1977;\x20
| Cole and Amman, 1980; McGregor and Cole, 1985; Hadley and\x20
| Veblen, 1993; Hadley, 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998; Hopkins,\x20
| 1909; Kulakowski et al., 2003). Stands with a significant loss of\x20
| overstory trees may also experience increased solar input\x20
| resulting in higher temperatures and decreased relative\x20
| humidity desiccating fuels and lowering fuel moisture content\x20
| (Brown, 1975). It has long been assumed that such changes in\x20
| fuel complexes increase the potential for fire occurrence, and\x20
| fire intensity and severity (Brown, 1975; Lotan et al., 1985).\x20
| Policies outlined in the National Fire Plan and the Healthy\x20
| Forest Restoration Act of 2003 assume that bark beetle\x20
| epidemics increase fire hazard for extended periods. It is true\x20
| that any factor that alters the quantity and/or quality of the fuels\x20
| complex potentially affects fire hazard. In reality, little research\x20
| provides specific, quantitative descriptions of bark beetle-
| caused modifications to forest fuels over time. Romme et al.\x20
| (2006) suggested that few scientific studies support the\x20
| assertion that insect outbreaks set the stage for severe forest\x20
| fires.\x20
| The majority of research to date has focused on the\x20
| influences of the mountain pine beetle on fuels accumulation\x20
| and subsequent wildfires in lodgepole pine (Pinus contorta\x20
| Dougl. ex Loud) forests (Brown, 1975; Geiszler et al., 1980; Armour, 1982; Lotan et al., 1985; Romme et al., 1986; Lynch\x20
| et al., 2006). Some research has investigated the spatial and\x20
| temporal relationships of historic spruce beetle outbreaks and\x20
| fires in Colorado (Bebi et al., 2003; Kulakowski et al., 2003;\x20
| Bigler et al., 2005; Kulakowski and Veblen, 2007). Research to\x20
| examine relationships between Douglas-fir beetle-caused tree\x20
| mortality and mixed severity wildfires is very limited.\x20
| Literature reviewed by Parker et al. (2006) presents an\x20
| uncritical acceptance of the hypothesis that fire exclusion is\x20
| the main cause of bark beetle outbreaks. The literature they\x20
| cite supports the contention that outbreaks contribute to a\x20
| buildup of standing and down fuels increasing fire occurrence\x20
| and severity. More empirical research has been done to\x20
| compare the extent of historic bark beetle outbreaks mapped\x20
| using aerial detection surveys. Kulakowski and Veblen (2007),\x20
| for example, used aerial detection survey maps and other\x20
| spatially explicit forest disturbance data to model the effect of\x20
| prior disturbances, including spruce beetle outbreaks, on\x20
| severity of wildfire in Colorado subalpine forests. They found\x20
| no difference in fire severity among stands with current\x20
| outbreaks and endemic stands. Using similar data layers,\x20
| Lynch et al. (2006) found that the odds of burning increased in\x20
| mountain pine beetle-killed stands in one of two outbreaks\x20
| studied following the Yellowstone fires of 1988. No significant\x20
| association was detected in the second mountain pine beetle\x20
| outbreak. Their data set was limited, however, because it did\x20
| not consider the historic stand conditions, and the temporal\x20
| and spatial variability of the beetle-influenced fuels stratum.\x20
| Lynch et al. (2006) indicated that the accuracy of aerial\x20
| detection survey data was more than sufficient for their\x20
| analysis. Kulakowski and Veblen (2007) suggested, however,\x20
| that errors inherent in aerial detection surveys of bark beetle\x20
| activity limit conclusions regarding the influence of bark\x20
| beetle outbreaks on fire extent and severity. They indicated\x20
| that future studies could benefit from methods that incorporate\x20
| ground surveys to better measure the extent of current and past\x20
| outbreak events. These studies are further confounded by\x20
| consideration of factors such as geography, climate and\x20
| disturbance history which make isolation of the cause and\x20
| effect relationship of fire/fuels/beetle factors difficult. Addi­
| tionally, they do not consider other important factors that\x20
| affect the spatial distribution of burned and unburned areas\x20
| such as real time fire weather.\x20
| Few studies for any species consist of fuels data collected in\x20
| actual outbreak areas or fires in bark beetle-affected fuels. In\x20
| this paper, we describe fire regimes in Intermountain Douglas-
| fir (Pseudotsuga menziesii var. glauca (Beissn.) Franco),\x20
| lodgepole pine, and Engelmann spruce (Picea engelmannii\x20
| Parry ex Engelm.)–subalpine fir (Abies lasiocarpa (Hook.)\x20
| Nutt.) forests and their associated bark beetle species. We use\x20
| actual fuels and stand data to describe changes in the fuels\x20
| complex that occur in bark beetle-affected stands in all three\x20
| species. We then use the fuels data to develop and describe\x20
| custom fuel models that predict fire behavior in endemic,\x20
| epidemic and post-epidemic stands. We compare and contrast\x20
| our data with existing research relating bark beetle-caused tree\x20
| mortality to fuels and potential changes in fire behavior. Finally,\x20

| 2. Disturbance regimes\x20
| The interplay between fuels, weather, and topography\x20
| primarily determines the pattern of fire in vegetation over time,\x20
| commonly called the fire regime (Pyne et al., 1996).\x20
| Topography is constant while fuels and weather can vary\x20
| substantially over time and space. The term ‘fuels’ refers to the\x20
| living and dead biomass that is burnable under certain moisture\x20
| conditions, which varies by forest type, time of year, and\x20
| disturbance history (Brown and Davis, 1973; Albini, 1976;\x20
| Pyne et al., 1996). Fuels are grouped by size classes which\x20
| affect surface area to volume ratios, and the fuels’ ability to\x20
| respond to changing moisture conditions. Smaller fuels are able\x20
| to respond to changes in atmospheric moisture more quickly\x20
| than larger fuels (Anderson, 1985). Weather, in terms of its\x20
| effects on fires, refers to both short-term phenomena such as\x20
| wind speed and relative humidity and long-term trends such as\x20
| drought and flooding (Schroeder and Buck, 1970). Throughout\x20
| the paper, we follow the definitions proposed by Hardy (2005)\x20
| for fire hazard and fire risk and fire severity. He considers fire\x20
| risk as the probability of ignition, fire hazard as the state of the\x20
| fuel, and fire severity as fire effects on wildland systems.\x20
| 2.1. Douglas-fir fire regime\x20
| Intermountain Douglas-fir forests typically occupy steep,\x20
| moist, northern aspects at mid elevations (1850–2500 m)\x20
| between the lower elevation ponderosa pine (Pinus ponderosa\x20
| Dougl. ex Laws) zone and higher elevation, mixed conifer\x20
| forests (Lanner, 1984; Harlow et al., 1979). This mid­
| elevational, vegetative gradient where Douglas-fir occurs has\x20
| a mixed severity fire regime characterized by both short interval\x20
| surface fires and long interval crown fires (Kilgore, 1981;\x20
| Wright and Bailey, 1982). Arno (1976) found that on the\x20
| Bitterroot National Forest surface fire return intervals ranged\x20
| from 15 to 30 years at lower elevation sites to 50 years on moist,\x20
| higher elevation sites. Crown fires occurred at intervals ranging\x20
| between 35 and 60 years (Arno, 1976). This variation in fire\x20
| frequency and intensity primarily depends upon specific site\x20
| conditions such as stand characteristics, habitat type, climate,\x20
| and fuel loads (Arno, 1980). Consequently, Douglas-fir can\x20
| form pure even-aged or uneven-aged stands across a variety of\x20
| habitat types (Mauk and Henderson, 1984). In uneven-aged\x20
| stands, common associates include subalpine and white fir\x20
| (Abies concolor (Gord. and Glend.) Lindl. ex Hildebr.), quaking\x20
| aspen (Populus tremuloides Michx.), ponderosa pine, limber\x20
| pine (Pinus flexilis James), and spruce (Picea sp.). Where fires\x20
| have occurred less frequently, individual Douglas-firs might be\x20
| up to 150 cm at diameter at breast height (dbh) (Lanner, 1984).\x20
| During the interval between stand-replacing fires, Douglas-
| fir stands are commonly subjected to wind, snow avalanches,\x20
| and Douglas-fir beetle infestations among other disturbances.\x20
| The interplay between disturbance agents often creates a\x20
| mosaic of even-aged patches across the landscape. As the age of an individual patch increases, it becomes increasingly\x20
| susceptible to Douglas-fir beetle activity especially where\x20
| recently fallen trees provide suitable habitat for colonization\x20
| and brood production (Rudinsky, 1966; Hebertson and Jenkins,\x20
| 2007). Coincident with the lengthening fire-free interval is an\x20
| increase in understory conifer establishment including Dou­
| glas-fir and true fir. In many Intermountain Douglas-fir stands,\x20
| western spruce budworm, Choristorneura occidentalis Free­
| man (Lepidoptera: Tortricidae), activity ranges from periodic to\x20
| chronic (Wulf and Cates, 1987). Budworm outbreaks are\x20
| exacerbated in multistoried stands and can decrease Douglas-fir\x20
| resistance to bark beetles and contribute to fuel changes\x20
| (Fredricks and Jenkins, 1988). Douglas-fir beetle populations\x20
| can also increase following low-intensity surface fires where\x20
| the insects are more likely to colonize fire-damaged trees\x20
| (Cunningham et al., 2005; Parker et al., 2006). As beetle-caused\x20
| tree mortality increases, the quantity and quality of the fuels\x20
| complex changes, increasing the potential fireline intensity of\x20
| subsequent fires. The increased intensity in older patches will\x20
| perpetuate the age class mosaic into future fire rotations.\x20
| 2.2. Lodgepole pine fire regime\x20
| Lodgepole pine forests in western North America typify fire\x20
| dependent ecosystems (Agee, 1993). These forests become\x20
| increasingly flammable with time and traits including cone\x20
| serotiny, volatile foliage, thin bark, high reproductive capacity,\x20
| and seedling survival evolved in response to fire (Lotan, 1976;\x20
| Schoennagel et al., 2003). With lodgepole pine forests\x20
| occurring at elevations ranging between 2500 and 3000 m,\x20
| however, local fire regimes can vary widely. For example, fire\x20
| return intervals in the northern Rockies may range from 200\x20
| years or more, to as few as 20 years with combinations of low-
| intensity surface fires and high-intensity crown fires (Arno,\x20
| 1980; Lotan et al., 1985; Barrett et al., 1997). Frequent and less\x20
| intense surface fires are more common in lower elevation\x20
| forests characterized by dry summers (Arno, 1980). At higher\x20
| elevations, fire return intervals lengthen and the potential for\x20
| high-intensity crown fires increases due to the cooler and\x20
| moister climate conditions (Arno, 1980; Romme, 1982). In\x20
| northern Utah, fire-free intervals ranged from 12 to 122 years\x20
| during pre-settlement times (Wadleigh and Jenkins, 1996).\x20
| Crown fire development in lodgepole pine stands depends upon\x20
| sufficient heat release from surface fuels, the height of the tree\x20
| crowns above the ground, and current weather conditions\x20
| (Bradley et al., 1992). In turn, these factors are dependent upon\x20
| specific stand conditions, locations, and fuel loadings. Many\x20
| even-aged lodgepole pine stands owe their origin to stand-
| replacing fires (Lotan et al., 1985).\x20
| Following stand-replacing fires, lodgepole pine quickly\x20
| colonizes burned sites (Lotan, 1976). After approximately 20\x20

| 2.3. Engelmann spruce/subalpine fir fire regime\x20
| A high-severity fire regime typically characterizes Inter­
| mountain spruce-fir forests (Agee, 1993; Edmonds et al., 2000).\x20
| Elevations exceed 2400 m in the spruce-fir zone and climates\x20
| are generally cool and moist in summer (Alexander, 1987).\x20
| These conditions primarily influence the flammability of fine\x20
| fuels reducing ignition risk and initial fire spread. Fire weather\x20
| conditions conducive to extreme fire behavior are also limited\x20
| to a few weeks in late summer during most years. As a\x20
| consequence, the frequency of wildfires in spruce-fir forests is\x20
| low with return intervals often greater than 100 years (Schmid\x20
| and Frye, 1977; Wright and Bailey, 1982; Alexander, 1987;\x20
| Baker and Veblen, 1990; Veblen et al., 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998). For example, Jenkins et al. (1998) determined that 200–\x20
| 400 years are needed for a spruce-fir climax forest to develop in\x20
| northern Utah, which generally agrees with other fire history\x20
| studies in the northern Rockies (Arno, 1980). Environments for\x20
| tree establishment within the spruce-fir zone are marginal\x20
| (Daniel and Schmidt, 1972; Paden et al., 1978; Knapp and\x20
| Smith, 1982; Alexander, 1987). Survival is especially low on\x20
| burned sites where extreme heat, frost, and browsing kill\x20
| unprotected Engelmann spruce seedlings. Consequently,\x20
| grasses and shrubs may dominate post-fire communities for\x20
| more than a century (Edmonds et al., 2000).\x20
| The fuels complex that develops in spruce-fir forests between\x20
| major wildfires creates the potential for crown fires rather than\x20
| surface fires (Taylor and Fonda, 1990). Trees with tightly\x20
| arranged crowns and low growing branches that are often\x20
| covered with lichens facilitate crown fire spread. Under dense,\x20
| closed canopy conditions, spruce-fir stands have, on average\x20
| sparse understories and a compact litter layer comprised of short,\x20
| fine needles that limit the potential for low-intensity surface fires\x20
| (Bradley et al., 1992; Swetnam and Baisan, 1996). Therefore,\x20
| many even-aged spruce-fir forests likely resulted from stand-
| replacing fires (Loope and Gruell, 1973).\x20
| During the relatively long period between major fire events,\x20
| spruce beetle-caused tree mortality has an important influence\x20
| on the vegetative dynamics and patterns of spruce-fir forests.\x20
| Following severe outbreaks, the return to climax spruce-
| dominated communities may require up to several hundred\x20
| years (Schmid and Frye, 1977; Baker and Veblen, 1990; Veblen\x20
| et al., 1991a; Veblen et al., 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998). With the\x20
| death of overstory spruce, advanced regeneration releases. The\x20
| majority of advanced regeneration, particularly on dry sites at\x20
| lower elevations is subalpine fir. The relatively small proportion\x20
| of advanced spruce regeneration in the understory of mature\x20
| stands can be attributed to the inability of spruce seedlings to\x20
| establish in heavy duff layers and several species of fungi that\x20
| specifically infect spruce seeds (Daniel and Schmidt, 1972;\x20
| Paden et al., 1978; Knapp and Smith, 1982; Alexander, 1987;\x20
| Aplet et al., 1988). Consequently, subalpine-fir typically\x20
| dominates stands for about 200 years until trees succumb to\x20
| the effects of disease (Veblen, 1986a,b; Aplet et al., 1988).\x20
| Dead and windthrown subalpine fir create canopy gaps and\x20
| upturned mineral soil that provide suitable habitat for spruce\x20
| seedling establishment (Shea, 1985; Aplet et al., 1988). Several\x20
| more decades may elapse before mature spruce dominates the\x20
| main canopy once again. Veblen et al. (1994) reported that\x20
| spruce-fir stands affected by the 1940s outbreak on the White\x20
| River Plateau, Colorado had a scarcity of spruce older than 140\x20
| years.\x20

| 3.1. Douglas-fir beetle\x20
| The Douglas-fir beetle occurs throughout the range of its\x20
| primary host, Douglas-fir (Schmitz and Gibson, 1996).\x20
| Endemic populations of beetles typically infest downed host\x20
| material and large diameter trees weakened by drought, fire,\x20
text: default page 6
| 21 M.J. Jenkins et al. / Forest Ecology and Management 254 (2008) 16–34 defoliation, and root diseases (Furniss, 1965; Coulson and\x20
| Witter, 1984; Christiansen et al., 1987; Fredricks and Jenkins,\x20
| 1988; Jenkins, 1990; Stark, 1993; Cunningham et al., 2005).\x20
| Populations have been known to reach outbreak levels\x20
| following disturbances that produce an abundance of green\x20
| host material (Lejeune et al., 1961; Hadley and Veblen, 1993).\x20
| Outbreaks also appear to coincide with prolonged periods of\x20
| drought.\x20
| Hadley (1994) found that the vulnerability of post-fire stands\x20
| to bark beetle attack was related to both aspect and topography.\x20
| Specifically, it appears that stands of Douglas-fir on north-
| facing slopes are more susceptible to beetle attack. This may be\x20
| due to the effects of fire suppression resulting in higher tree\x20
| densities and a more suitable distribution of large diameter trees\x20
| (Hadley and Veblen, 1993; Hadley, 1994). In the Intermountain\x20
| region, Douglas-fir beetle outbreaks are site specific depending\x20
| on the availability of susceptible hosts and mortality centers\x20
| may be confined to small groups of trees. Within highly\x20
| susceptible stands or landscapes, beetles may kill large groups\x20
| of trees (exceeding 100 trees) and result in 60–80% mortality of\x20
| the Douglas-fir over 30 cm in diameter (Furniss and Orr, 1978;\x20
| Amman and Ryan, 1991; Patterson, 1991; Schmitz and Gibson,\x20
| 1996). Larger diameter trees have thicker phloem necessary for\x20
| greater reproductive success (Lessard and Schmid, 1990;\x20
| Amman and Ryan, 1991). Bark beetles generally only attack\x20
| smaller diameter trees during epidemics.\x20
| Stands considered ‘‘high hazard’’ for a Douglas-fir beetle\x20
| outbreak have average Douglas-fir diameters greater than 23 cm\x20
| dbh, average age of Douglas-fir in the stand greater than 120\x20
| years, stocking densities exceeding 28 m2/ha, and more than a\x20
| 50% Douglas-fir component. Outbreaks of Douglas-fir beetles\x20
| typically last 2–4 years, but periods of unusually dry weather\x20
| (Schmitz and Gibson, 1996) or budworm defoliation (Fredericks\x20
| and Jenkins, 1988) may prolong activity in certain areas.\x20
| 3.2. Mountain pine beetle\x20
| The mountain pine beetle is the most aggressive bark beetle\x20
| in western North America. In the Intermountain West, this\x20
| insect primarily infests and kills lodgepole pine, ponderosa\x20
| pine, white bark pine (Pinus albicaulis Engelm.), limber pine,\x20
| and other western white pines throughout their range. During\x20
| severe outbreaks, mountain pine beetles may also attack\x20
| Douglas-fir, true firs, and spruce, although successful brood\x20
| production in these species is rare (Furniss and Carolin, 1980).\x20
| When populations are low, beetles infest weakened and injured\x20
| trees. Drought stress often contributes to the initiation of\x20
| mountain pine beetle outbreaks (Mattson and Haack, 1987;\x20
| Goheen and Hansen, 1993). Mountain pine beetle spread occurs\x20
| most rapidly in old, dense stands comprised of a majority of\x20
| large lodgepole pines. The most susceptible stands have trees\x20
| over 80 years old, average tree diameters greater than 20 cm\x20
| dbh, an average phloem thickness greater than 0.254 cm, over\x20
| 18 m2/ha mean basal area, and stand density index between 140\x20
| and 245 (Cole and Amman, 1980; Berryman, 1982; Amman\x20
| et al., 1985; McGregor and Cole, 1985; Safranyik, 1989;\x20
| Anhold et al., 1996). During outbreaks, beetles infest the older, large diameter trees first, and eventually kill smaller trees as\x20
| populations build (McGregor and Cole, 1985). Between 1998\x20
| and 2005, mountain pine beetles killed approximately 27\x20
| million lodgepole over 149,734 ha in the Sawtooth National\x20
| Recreation Area, Idaho (USDA FS, 2006). Epidemics usually\x20
| collapse with the loss of all or most large trees.\x20
| 3.3. Spruce beetle\x20
| The spruce beetle is the most important biotic disturbance\x20
| agent affecting post-fire, spruce-fir forests in the Intermountain\x20
| West (Veblen et al., 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998). Engelmann\x20
| spruce serves as the principle host for spruce beetles, although\x20
| they may attack Colorado blue spruce (Picea pungens Engelm.)\x20
| and lodgepole pine during epidemics (Miller, 1970; Schmid and\x20
| Frye, 1977). Endemic populations of spruce beetles typically\x20
| reproduce within newly fallen trees (Schmid, 1981). Occa­
| sionally they will also attack large, overmature spruce, killing\x20
| trees individually or in small groups creating canopy gaps\x20
| (Veblen et al., 1991b; Goyer et al., 1998). Local populations can\x20
| build to epidemic levels following stand disturbances that\x20
| produce an abundance of suitable host material (Schmid and\x20
| Frye, 1977; Jenkins et al., 1998; Hebertson and Jenkins, 2007).\x20
| As competition for food intensifies, beetles may then attack\x20
| standing live trees. Stand conditions, host vigor, climate, and\x20
| existing population levels also influence the initiation of\x20
| outbreaks and the spread of spruce beetles through living stands\x20
| (Schmid and Frye, 1976; Schmid, 1981; Hard, 1985; Werner\x20
| and Holsten, 1985; Paine et al., 1997; Jenkins et al., 1998;\x20
| Holsten et al., 1999; Hansen et al., 2001; Hebertson and\x20
| Jenkins, 2007). Dense stands comprised of over 65% large\x20
| diameter spruce (greater than 40 cm dbh) are most susceptible\x20
| to spruce beetle attack. Recent spruce beetle outbreaks in Utah\x20
| have resulted in tree mortality exceeding 1.5 million spruce\x20
| trees and the loss of over 90% of the large spruce component\x20
| (Dymerski et al., 2001; Keyes et al., 2003; USDA FS, 2006).\x20

| 5. Fire behavior in bark beetle-affected conifer fuels\x20
| 5.1. Fire behavior\x20
| Section 4 describes the general effects of bark beetles on\x20

| elevations in the subalpine zone (Bessie and Johnson, 1995).\x20
| Fire behavior is described in terms of its rate of spread, flame\x20
| length, and intensity and can be predicted if appropriate inputs\x20
| of fuels, moisture contents, slope, and wind speed are available\x20
| (Rothermel, 1983). Although fire behavior inputs are readily measured and outputs can be accurately derived, the relative\x20
| importance of inputs on ecosystem effects is poorly understood\x20

| 5.5. Crown fire relationships\x20
| The potential transition from a surface fire to a passive\x20
| crown is determined by a critical intensity of the surface fire and\x20
| the crown base height that provides a vertically continuous fuel\x20
| stratum of sufficiently low fuel moisture. This phenomenon is\x20
| commonly called torching in ladder fuels. The potential\x20
| transition from passive to active crown fire is determined by\x20
| canopy closure or canopy bulk density, foliar moisture content,\x20
| and effective wind speed. Rothermel (1991) developed models\x20
| that defined the above variables and used foliar moisture inputs\x20
| from Van Wagner (1977) to predict crown fire initiation and\x20

| 5.6. Ecological effects of extreme fire behavior\x20
| The timing of high-intensity crown fires may influence post-

| mountain pine beetle outbreaks contribute to pine forest\x20
| flammability and enhance the opportunity for serotinous\x20

| Despain (1990), however, put forth the hypothesis that\x20
| mountain pine beetle outbreaks actually decrease the risk of\x20

| cause changes to the structure of the forest such that fires are\x20
| inhibited. He noted examples in Yellowstone National Park\x20
| where fires went around previous mountain pine beetle\x20
| outbreaks, although admitted that his observations were merely\x20
| anecdotal in nature and not specifically studied. Despain (1990)\x20
| concluded that further work should be done to better understand\x20

| (1986) also concluded that due to decreases in continuity of\x20

| (after outbreak) may be lower.\x20
| While the accumulation of fuels and the presence of\x20

| 6. Bark beetle management strategies and implications\x20
| for fuels and fire behavior\x20
| Land managers often employ various prevention, suppres­
| sion and restoration strategies to mitigate potentially adverse\x20
| impacts resulting from bark beetle outbreaks. Prevention\x20
| strategies often utilize silvicultural practices including thinning\x20
| to modify stand conditions favorable to insect and disease\x20
| agents and should occur before populations reach unmanage­
| able levels. After the initiation of an outbreak, management\x20
| options become increasingly limited and delays in treatment\x20
| greatly reduce opportunities for success (Fettig et al., 2007). By\x20
| enhancing stand diversity and resiliency, prevention strategies\x20
| can help avoid unacceptable losses of valuable resources,\x20
| maintain or enhance resource objectives, and maximize\x20
| revenue in the long term (Schmid and Frye, 1977; Cahill,\x20
| 1978; Cole et al., 1983; McGregor et al., 1987; Amman et al.,\x20
| 1988a,b; Fettig et al., 2007; Schowalter et al., 1992; Anhold\x20
| et al., 1996; Schmitz and Gibson, 1996; Holsten et al., 1999).\x20
| Suppression strategies are usually implemented with the\x20
| detection of building bark beetle populations. These strategies\x20
| include sanitation and salvage treatments, insecticide applica­
| tions and the use of pheromones to reduce local beetle\x20
| populations and protect high value trees from bark beetle attack\x20
| in recreation sites. When implemented during the initial stages\x20
| of an outbreak, suppression activities can reduce population\x20
| levels and the distribution and extent of bark beetle-caused tree\x20
| mortality. Treatments associated with suppression alternatives,\x20
| however, are usually limited, often occurring at small scales.\x20
| Because treatment alternatives do not modify stand conditions\x20
| at large scales, the benefits are often short term. Restoration\x20
| activities attempt to reestablish vegetation and promote the\x20
| long-term resiliency of forests to insect and disease outbreaks\x20
| or attacks.\x20
| Reducing the accumulation of hazardous fuels and/or the\x20
| potential for extreme fire behavior resulting from bark beetle\x20
| mortality is often cited as a rationale for implementing bark\x20
| beetle management treatments (Schott, 1994; Gorte, 1996;\x20
| USDA Forest Service, 1998). Romme et al. (2006) suggested\x20
| that there is little ecological justification in removing insect-
| killed trees. Additionally, slash produced during bark beetle\x20
| treatments and post-harvest blow down will result in fuel\x20
| complexes different from those in either bark beetle-killed or\x20
| uninfested stands. Conventional slash fuel models (Rothermel,\x20
| 1983) used to estimate fire behavior in stands treated for bark\x20
| beetles suggest increased potential for ignition and alteration\x20
| of fire behavior depending on the nature of the treatment\x20
| (Turner et al., 1994; DellaSala et al., 1995).\x20
| To determine how fuels might differ in spruce-fir forests\x20
| managed for spruce beetle versus unmanaged forests, we\x20
| measured fuels in thinned and control stands on the Fishlake\x20
| National Forest in south central Utah (Jenkins et al., 2005).\x20
| Moderately thinned stands had residual basal areas ranging between 23 and 28.4 m2/ha with average spruce diameters of\x20
| 30.5 cm dbh. Heavily thinned stands had residual basal areas of\x20
| 13.8–18.3 m2/ha with average spruce diameters of 25.4 cm dbh.\x20
| Fuels measurements were then used to create custom fuel\x20
| models to compare potential fire behavior among the\x20
| treatments. Fine fuels (1, 10, and 100 h size classes) increased\x20
| in treated stands with the greatest amount of total fuel\x20
| accumulating in the heavily thinned plots. Moderately thinned\x20
| plots generally had the lowest fuel depths. Heavily thinned\x20
| plots had a greater average fuel depth compared to the control\x20
| plot in some stands. The 1000 h fuels were quite variable from\x20
| treatment to treatment in the areas sampled. As expected, the\x20
| amount of available crown fuel significantly dropped in each of\x20
| the thinning treatments. Crown bulk densities were also\x20
| significantly lower for the thinning treatments.\x20
| Predicted fire behavior was greatest in heavily thinned\x20
| stands where the increase in fuel depth, the amount of fuel,\x20
| along with a higher mid-flame wind speed increased potential\x20
| fire behavior. In other areas, the greatest fire behavior was in the\x20
| control plots associated with the highest wind speed. Although\x20
| more fuel was available in the heavily thinned stands and the\x20
| mid-flame wind speed was greater, the fuel depth was less than\x20
| that of the control. This was due to the mechanically compacted\x20
| fuel bed which does not support combustion as well as the more\x20

| result.\x20
| Fire behavior in the heavily thinned plots, however,\x20
| was probably under-predicted because the fuel moistures used\x20
| did not take into account the effect of increased solar radia­
| tion reaching the exposed fuel bed. When compared to the\x20
| heavily thinned and the control for both areas, the moderately\x20
| thinned plots had intermediate fire behavior characteristics. In\x20
| terms of crown fires, the control plots had the greatest potential\x20
| to sustain a crown fire of some kind (i.e. lowest crown fire\x20
| index).\x20
| These results demonstrate that fuels and consequent fire\x20
| behavior resulting from spruce beetle management strategies\x20

| thinning treatments and influences on fire behavior in western\x20

| Skinner, 2005). Moderate thinnings may result in less potential\x20
| for extreme behavior compared to unmanaged stands. Greater\x20
| fuel depths, mid-flame wind speeds and lower fuel moistures in\x20
| heavily treated stands (>60% basal area reduction) might\x20
| increase potential fire behavior compared to unmanaged stands.\x20

| thoroughly consider post-harvest fuel treatments to reduce\x20
| surface fuels. Thinning followed by sufficient treatment of\x20
| surface fuels usually outweighs changes in fire weather factors\x20
| (wind speed and fuel moisture) resulting in an overall reduction\x20
| in expected fire behavior (Weatherspoon, 1996). Due to\x20
| similarities in bark beetle-created fuels, this consideration\x20
| would apply to management strategies implemented for other\x20
| bark beetle species as well.\x20

| 7.1. Fuels management strategies\x20
| With growing concern over the occurrence of uncharacter­
| istic wildfires in the western United States, federal and state\x20
| land management agencies have initiated aggressive fuels\x20
| reduction programs to reduce the risk, extent and severity of\x20
| wildfires, particularly in wildland/urban interfaces (Agee and\x20
| Skinner, 2005; Glitzenstein et al., 2006). Fuels management\x20
| practices often include prescribed fire or mechanical treatments\x20
| that act as fire substitutes or are used to compliment prescribed\x20
| burn programs (Glitzenstein et al., 2006). Implementation of\x20
| these treatments, however, can injure residual trees predispos­
| ing them to attack by bark beetles (Ryan and Amman, 1994;\x20
| Rippy et al., 2005; Arno, 2000; McCullough et al., 1998).\x20
| Increased amounts of host material (slash and stumps) and host\x20
| volatiles in treated stands can also attract bark beetles (Schmid,\x20
| 1977; Amman et al., 1988b; Six et al., 2002; Fettig et al., 2006).\x20
| Much recent research has consequently investigated the\x20
| influence of fuels treatments on the behavior of certain bark\x20
| beetle species (Six et al., 2002; Wallin et al., 2003; Fettig et al.,\x20
| 2006).\x20
| General recommendations to minimize bark beetle activity\x20
| associated with fuels treatments resulting from these studies\x20
| include (1) avoid implementing treatments concomitant with\x20
| adult beetle flight periods, (2) remove slash and all susceptible\x20
| non-merchantable host material from the site, or treat slash on\x20
| site using pile-and-burn, lop-and-scatter, or chipping methods,\x20
| (3) remove or burn any infested host material, (4) limit the\x20
| diameter and length of host material, and (5) avoid thinning in\x20
| stands highly susceptible to root diseases (Six et al., 2002;\x20
| Steed and Wagner, 2004; Rippy et al., 2005; Fettig et al., 2006).\x20
| Chipping versus lop and scatter fuel treatments may exacerbate\x20
| bark beetle attacks in some bark beetle–host systems (Fettig\x20
| et al., 2006). Where chipping is the preferable treatment\x20
| method, Fettig et al. (2006) suggested allowing slash to dry\x20
| before treatment and limiting the amount of chips around the\x20
| bases of residual trees. With the inability to treat slash using one\x20
| of the above methods, funnel traps baited with aggregation\x20
| pheromones and placed around piles can capture a significant\x20
| number of emerging adult beetles (Ross and Daterman, 1997).\x20
| Regardless of treatment, it is important for land managers to\x20
| assess general beetle population levels and beetle-caused tree\x20
| mortality prior to treatment and monitor treated areas regularly.\x20
| 7.2. Delayed tree mortality following fire\x20
| Less obvious is the fact that fire can wound trees and render\x20
| them susceptible to bark beetles that in turn can kill trees\x20
| altering the quantity and quality of the fuels complex. Bark\x20
| beetle outbreaks following fires are not unprecedented, but\x20
| neither are they certain. Beetle outbreaks following fires have\x20
| been documented in the Intermountain West. Douglas-fir\x20
| beetle, spruce beetle, and pine engraver beetle (Ips pini Say\x20
| (Coleoptera: Curculionidae) outbreaks following wildfires in 1988 and 1994 became extensive, damaging, and long-lasting\x20
| in parts of Montana, Idaho and Yellowstone National Park\x20
| (Ryan and Amman, 1996).\x20
| Prior to implementing post-fire salvage and rehabilitation\x20
| treatments land managers often need to know which trees will\x20
| likely die from fire damage, which might survive fire effects but\x20
| subsequently be killed by bark beetles (Parker et al., 2006), and\x20
| which others may be more susceptible to fungal infections and\x20
| degradation (DeNitto et al., 2000). Bark beetles frequently\x20
| attack fire-injured trees although their direct effect on tree\x20
| mortality remains unclear (Ryan and Reinhardt, 1988).\x20
| Following the Greater Yellowstone fires in 1988 for example,\x20
| Rasmussen et al. (1996) found that fire damage accounted for\x20
| the majority of delayed conifer mortality, although bark beetles\x20
| contributed to some mortality and ultimately changes in the\x20
| mosaic of fire-killed, green trees. In other studies of those same\x20
| fires, spruce beetle attacks were associated with fire-damaged\x20
| Engelmann spruce, however mountain pine beetle showed little\x20
| attraction to fire-damaged lodgepole pine (Ryan and Amman,\x20
| 1994, 1996). Weatherby et al. (1994) observed that Douglas-fir\x20
| beetles killed a significant number of large diameter Douglas-
| firs that had significantly less damage than fire-killed trees\x20
| following the Lowman (Idaho) fire of 1989. Hood and Bentz\x20
| (2007) also found that Douglas-fir beetles had a significant\x20
| influence on post-fire, delayed Douglas-fir mortality, killing\x20
| trees that would have otherwise survived wildfires occurring in\x20
| Montana and Wyoming during 2000 and 2001.\x20
| The complex interactions between the physiological\x20
| condition of host trees pre- and post-fire, the season of burn,\x20
| the amount and severity of tree damage, the nature of bark\x20
| beetle populations, weather and climate conditions, other biotic\x20
| and abiotic factors make accurate predictions of post-fire tree\x20
| mortality difficult (DeNitto et al., 2000). For individual\x20
| lodgepole pine, Douglas-fir and Engelmann spruce injured\x20
| during the Greater Yellowstone fires, bark beetle attacks were\x20
| strongly and positively correlated with the percentage of basal\x20
| area circumference killed (Rasmussen et al., 1996). Douglas-fir\x20
| beetles preferentially attacked large diameter Douglas-fir trees\x20
| with 60–80% bole char and crown volume scorch, and a 50–\x20
| 70% likelihood of mortality due to fire 1 year after a ground fire\x20
| on Beaver Mountain (Utah) in 1994 (Cunningham et al., 2005).\x20
| Similar levels of crown volume scorch and cambium death were\x20
| associated with 95% of Douglas-fir beetle attacked trees\x20
| following the Montana and Wyoming fires of 2000 and 2001,\x20

| important for attack than fire injury (Hood and Bentz, 2007).\x20
| Depending upon the time of burn, bark beetles may attack\x20
| fire-injured trees until they recover, or their phloem becomes\x20
| unsuitable for habitation (as long as four years post-burn)\x20

| production of broods in fire-injured trees may increase the risk\x20

| 8. Bark beetle, fuels, and fire management strategies\x20
| Policies including the National Fire Plan and the Healthy\x20
| Forests Restoration Act of 2003 demonstrate that the\x20
| development of resilient, fire-dependent forests is a national\x20
| emphasis (Jain and Graham, 2004). This emphasis may soon\x20
| encompass high-elevation subalpine forests as global climate\x20
| change increases the potential for bark beetle outbreaks and\x20
| intense wildfires, wildland/urban interfaces expand, and\x20
| demands on important forest resources rise (Logan and Powell,\x20
| 2001; Williams and Liebold, 2002; Carroll et al., 2003; Gan,\x20
| 2003; Breshears et al., 2005; Brooks et al., 2006).\x20
| As this paper indicates, the interaction of western bark\x20
| beetles, fuels and fire in forest systems is inherently complex\x20
| and much remains unknown. This presents unique challenges\x20
| for land managers, particularly in light of competing social\x20
| values, multiple stakeholder interests, and uncertain manage­
| ment outcomes (Graham et al., 2004; Brooks et al., 2006;\x20
| Snider et al., 2006). Additionally, differences in the physical\x20
| environment, stand conditions, the amount and distribution of\x20
| available fuels, and weather make ‘one size fits all’ manage­
| ment approaches ineffective (Graham et al., 2004). The\x20
| development of management plans that give greater considera­
| tion to these interactions, however, can provide decision-
| makers with better guidance for meeting important resource\x20
| objectives, reducing treatment costs, minimizing adverse\x20
| ecological impacts, and avoiding potential controversy and\x20
| litigation.\x20
| Identifying and explicitly defining the most important short\x20
| and long-term resource objectives of the landscape is vital to the success of any management plan. Based on these\x20
| definitions, land managers can determine the extent of\x20
| landscapes requiring treatment, develop ecologically appro­
| priate and feasible treatment alternatives, decide where and\x20
| when treatments would be most efficacious and over what\x20
| amount of time (Agee and Skinner, 2005). Multiple resource\x20
| objectives often necessitate that management strategies\x20
| prioritize landscapes for treatment. Land managers should\x20
| not consider every landscape with hazardous bark beetle or\x20
| fuels conditions a high priority candidate for treatment\x20
| (Schoennagel et al., 2004; Agee and Skinner, 2005). For\x20
| example, lower elevation drier forest types might receive higher\x20
| priority for large-scale treatments than higher elevation forest\x20
| types (Agee and Skinner, 2005). The immediate implementa­
| tion of large-scale treatments in wildland/urban interfaces,\x20
| however, may be appropriate in all forest types (Glitzenstein\x20
| et al., 2006). Other treatment priorities may include landscapes\x20
| with high economic investment, commercial or recreation\x20
| values, or critical wildlife habitat. Prioritization should finally\x20
| consider landscapes where treatments can best enhance long-
| term sustainability and resiliency to change (Agee and Skinner,\x20
| 2005).\x20
| The mitigation of potentially adverse bark beetles and fire\x20
| effects is maximized when treatments occur at landscape scales\x20
| and integrate the spatial arrangement of forest types, stand\x20
| conditions, treatment units and prescriptions (Graham et al.,\x20
| 2004; Agee and Skinner, 2005; Fettig et al., 2007). Small-scale,\x20
| scattered treatments do not effectively reduce the long-term\x20
| susceptibility of forests to bark beetle infestation or fragment\x20
| the landscape fuels (Samman and Logan, 2000; Graham et al.,\x20
| 2004; Agee and Skinner, 2005; Fettig et al., 2007). Failure to\x20
| reduce susceptibility across the landscape and/or address the\x20
| entire bark beetle populations often results in insect spread\x20
| from untreated stands into treated areas with the depletion of\x20
| host resources (Fettig et al., 2007). With extreme fire weather\x20
| conditions, intense fires can also overwhelm small-scale\x20
| treatments (Agee and Skinner, 2005). Even with a reduction\x20
| in fire behavior, treatments with substantial edge adjacent to\x20
| untreated units are likely to suffer substantial tree mortality\x20
| (Agee and Skinner, 2005).\x20
| 9. Future research direction\x20
| Recent and on-going research has provided greater insight\x20
| into the nature of bark beetle mortality, associated fuels\x20
| accumulation, and fire interactions in Intermountain Douglas-
| fir, lodgepole pine and Engelmann spruce forest types. Similar\x20
| research is needed to elucidate these relationships for these\x20
| species in other regions, as well as in western ponderosa pine\x20
| forest types and pinyon-juniper woodlands.\x20
| Many effects of bark beetle-caused injury and mortality on\x20
| fuels and fire behavior remain uncertain. Adult bark beetles and\x20
| larval feeding in conjunction with the introduction and\x20
| colonization of blue-staining fungi (Leptographium spp.)\x20
| disrupt the transport of water to the foliage of host trees\x20
| (Cates and Alexander, 1982). Foliar discoloration indicative of\x20
| needle death typically becomes apparent 1 year after bark\x20
text: default page 14
| 29 M.J. Jenkins et al. / Forest Ecology and Management 254 (2008) 16–34 beetle attack. Dead needles have low moisture content and\x20
| strongly influence the potential for crown fire initiation and\x20
| rates of crown fire spread. Little is known about the moisture\x20
| content of green needles on current mass attacked trees and how\x20
| initial changes in moisture content might contribute to crown\x20
| fire potential and intensity.\x20
| Custom fuel models used to predict fire behavior in bark\x20
| beetle-affected landscapes indicate that fire severity increases\x20
| as snags begin to fall. Limited research suggests that fall rates\x20
| of bark beetle-killed trees are highly variable or largely\x20
| unknown for many Intermountain forest types. Reliable\x20
| estimates of fall rates also require further study to determine\x20
| the influence of soil and site characteristics, wind, and root\x20
| diseases.\x20
| Current models of bark beetle-affected fuels assume\x20
| homogeneous tree mortality across the landscape. In reality,\x20
| patterns (spatial arrangement and distribution) of bark beetle\x20
| tree mortality and fuels accumulations exhibit considerable\x20
| variability across the landscape depending on existing stand\x20
| conditions, local bark beetle population levels, site character­
| istics, and the distribution of decay, and root disease centers\x20
| (Cobb, 1989; Lundquist, 2007). The development of fire spread\x20
| models in various bark beetle-affected landscapes may help\x20
| land managers determine how patterns of bark beetle mortality\x20
| may decrease or exacerbate (prolong) hazardous fuels (fire\x20
| hazards) and consequently facilitate the identification of stands\x20
| to prioritize for treatment.\x20
| There are currently several new modeling tools including\x20
| Landscape Fire and Resource Management Planning Tools\x20
| Project (LANDFIRE) and the Fire and Fuels Extension to the\x20
| Forest Vegetation Simulator (FFE-FVS) available to fire and\x20
| fuels managers to aid in fuel treatment placement and\x20
| verification. Due to the nature of the data acquisition techniques\x20
| in these programs, there is often inconsistency between the\x20
| actual fuel conditions and conventional default fuel conditions\x20
| provided by the programs (Anderson, 1982; Johnson and\x20
| Peterson, 2005; Scott and Burgan, 2005). The use of site-
| specific fuels data inputs improves the reliability of modeled\x20
| (fire behavior) projections, however the collection of these data\x20
| is often costly and labor intensive. The development of tools\x20
| including photo appraisal guides for bark beetle-affected fuels\x20
| would facilitate collecting large amounts of actual surface fuels\x20
| data for modeling purposes.\x20
| 10. Conclusions\x20
| Bark beetles and their effects on fuel accumulations and\x20
| subsequent fire hazard are poorly understood. Clearly, bark\x20
| beetles cause tree damage and/or mortality that can alter fuels\x20
| complexes of lodgepole pine, Douglas-fir and Engelmann\x20
| spruce forest types, among others, and consequently associated\x20
| fire behavior. Mortality caused by Douglas-fir beetle, mountain\x20
| pine beetle and spruce beetle species can contribute significant\x20
| amounts of large woody debris to the fuels complex (Lotan\x20
| et al., 1985; Romme et al., 1986; McCullough et al., 1998).\x20
| However, a period of 20 or more years after the outbreak may be\x20
| necessary before the majority of beetle-killed trees are on the forest floor (Mielke, 1950). Initially after the outbreak, there are\x20
| substantial increases in the amount of fine dead fuels in the\x20
| canopy. The majority of these fuels remain in the canopy for 2–\x20
| 3 years after the outbreak (Knight, 1987; Schmid and Amman,\x20
| 1992; Page and Jenkins, 2007a). Immediately following the\x20
| outbreak, there may be a large increase in the amount of\x20
| understory shrub and herbaceous plants (Reid, 1989; Stone and\x20
| Wolfe, 1996; Page and Jenkins, 2007a). With an abundance of\x20
| green understory shrub and herbaceous plants there may be a\x20
| decrease in potential surface fire behavior (Stocks, 1987). The\x20
| large amount of fuels present due to beetle mortality may not\x20
| represent abnormal fuel conditions in high elevation systems\x20
| (Brown, 1983; Johnson et al., 2001; Schoennagel et al., 2004).\x20
| In the case of spruce beetle, some research indicates that fuel\x20
| accumulations caused by the spruce beetle do not increase the\x20
| density of fires in a given area when compared to unaffected\x20
| stands (Bebi et al., 2003; Kulakowski et al., 2003). Over\x20
| decades, when there is a significant component of large down\x20
| wood, there will be an increase in fire severity during high-
| intensity fire events.\x20
| Although bark beetles have significant effects on fuels,\x20
| weather conditions play an important role in fires in subalpine\x20
| forests (Bessie and Johnson, 1995). Conifer forests experien­
| cing extreme fire weather are subject to intense fires under\x20
| many fuel conditions, while stands with high fuel loadings will\x20
| only have an affect on crown fire initiation under moderate fire\x20
| weather.\x20
| Depending on pre-existing stand conditions bark beetle\x20
| treatments such as moderate thinnings may result in less\x20
| potential for extreme behavior compared to unmanaged stands.\x20
| Greater fuel depths, mid-flame wind speeds and lower fuel\x20
| moistures in heavily treated stands might increase potential fire\x20
| behavior compared to unmanaged stands. This necessitates\x20
| bark beetle management strategies more thoroughly consider\x20
| post-harvest fuel treatments to reduce surface fuels to lower the\x20
| risk of severe wildfire.\x20
| Fuels reduction treatments may result in residual tree\x20
| damage and/or the production of host material (green slash and\x20
| stumps) and volatiles that can attract and concentrate certain\x20
| bark beetle species, particularly if treatments coincide with\x20
| beetle flight periods. Land managers can mitigate for bark\x20
| beetle population increases by avoiding treatments during bark\x20
| beetle flight, minimizing activities in those portions of stands\x20
| where decay and root diseases are prevalent, and managing\x20
| green slash.\x20
| Bark beetles frequently attack fire-injured trees and\x20
| contribute to delayed tree mortality. Several models have been\x20
| developed that provide land managers with the best predictors\x20
| for the post-fire Douglas-fir mortality. Whether bark beetle\x20
| populations build in fire-damaged trees and contribute to\x20
| outbreaks remains uncertain. Land managers may determine\x20
| the likelihood of bark beetle depredations using assessments of\x20
| stand conditions integrated with burn intensities for bark beetle\x20
| species of concern.\x20
| Management plans that consider bark beetle/fire interactions\x20
| can provide decision-makers with better guidance for meeting\x20
| important resource objectives, reducing treatment costs,\x20
text: default page 15
| 30 M.J. Jenkins et al. / Forest Ecology and Management 254 (2008) 16–34 minimizing adverse ecological impacts, and avoiding potential\x20
| controversy. Management plans that explicitly define both short\x20
| and long-term resource objectives enable land managers to\x20
| devise the most appropriate treatment strategies. The mitigation\x20
| of potentially adverse bark beetle and fire effects is maximized\x20
| when treatments occur at landscape scales and integrate the\x20
| spatial arrangement of forest types and stand conditions.\x20

| Received 6 June 2007; received in revised form 4 September 2007; accepted 5 September 2007 Abstract Bark beetle-caused tree mortality in conifer forests affects the quantity and quality of forest fuels and has long been assumed to increase fire\x20
| hazard and potential fire behavior. In reality, bark beetles, and their effects on fuel accumulation, and subsequent fire hazard, are poorly understood.\x20
| We extensively sampled fuels in three bark beetle-affected Intermountain conifer forests and compared these data to existing research on bark\x20
| beetle/fuels/fire interactions within the context of the disturbance regime. Data were collected in endemic, epidemic and post-epidemic stands of\x20
| Douglas-fir, lodgepole pine and Engelmann spruce. From these data, we evaluated the influence of bark beetle-caused tree mortality on various\x20
| fuels characteristics over the course of a bark beetle rotation. The data showed that changes in fuels over time create periods where the potential for\x20
| high intensity and/or severe fires increases or decreases. The net result of bark beetle epidemics was a substantial change in species composition\x20
| and a highly altered fuels complex. Early in epidemics there is a net increase in the amount of fine surface fuels when compared to endemic stands.\x20
| In post-epidemic stands large, dead, woody fuels, and live surface fuels dominate. We then discuss potential fire behavior in bark beetle-affected\x20
| conifer fuels based on actual and simulated fuels data. Results indicated that for surface fires both rates of fire spread and fireline intensities were\x20
| higher in the current epidemic stands than in the endemic stands. Rates of spread and fireline intensities were higher in epidemic stands due,\x20
| however, to decreased vegetative sheltering and its effect on mid-flame wind speed, rather than changes in fuels. Passive crown fires were more\x20
| likely in post-epidemic stands, but active crown fires were less likely due to decreased aerial fuel continuity. We also discuss the ecological effects\x20
| of extreme fire behavior. Information is presented on managing forests to reduce the impact of bark beetle outbreaks and the interplay between\x20
| management, bark beetle populations, fuels and fire hazard and behavior.\x20

| disturbance in Intermountain Forests over varying temporal and spatial scales. 1. Introduction\x20
| The interactions among disturbances operating over various\x20
| spatial and temporal scales determine the nature of forested\x20
| landscapes. In some western forest systems, for example fire-
| dependent lodgepole pine forests, the influence of a single\x20
| disturbance agent, such as wildfire, is so pervasive that it\x20
| dominates forest dynamics. In most low to mid-elevation\x20
| ponderosa pine forests, the relatively rapid accumulation of\x20
| fuels and weather conditions required for fire ignition and\x20
| spread make seasonal fires common. Fire return intervals from\x20
| as few as 5 up to 100 or more years are capable of driving forest\x20
| succession (Jenkins et al., 1998). In high elevation, subalpine\x20
| forests, lightning is relatively common and sufficient amounts\x20
| of fuels accumulate to sustain fire. However, short snow-free\x20
| periods, low temperatures, and high relative humidities often\x20
| preclude fire occurrence for extended periods (Arno, 2000). In\x20
| these forest systems, Dendroctonus bark beetles (Coleoptera:\x20
| Curculionidae, Scolytinae) coupled with other widespread\x20
| disturbance events such as harvesting, snow avalanches and\x20
| wind throw primarily regulate forest dynamics (Veblen et al.,\x20
| 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998). A conceptual model of the\x20
| interactions of agents of disturbance over large spatial and long\x20
| temporal scales is presented in Fig. 1.\x20
| Bark beetles naturally occur throughout the ranges of their\x20
| host trees and have a critical role within healthy, functioning\x20
| ecosystems (Roe and Amman, 1970; Geiszler et al., 1980; Gara\x20
| et al., 1985; Veblen et al., 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998; Bebi et al.,\x20
| 2003; Kulakowski et al., 2003). During endemic population\x20
| phases, bark beetles typically infest blowdown and trees\x20
| weakened by fire, diseases, and drought (Schmid, 1981; Raffa\x20
| and Berryman, 1983; Mattson and Haack, 1987; Goheen and\x20
| Hansen, 1993; Cunningham et al., 2005). Periodically, popula­
| tions of bark beetles reach outbreak levels infesting otherwise\x20
| healthy trees (Raffa, 2001; Samman and Logan, 2000).\x20
| The level of bark beetle activity fluctuates with the\x20
| availability of suitable host material, stand conditions,\x20
| environmental factors, and the abundance of parasites and\x20
| predators. Although many factors may contribute to the\x20
| initiation of outbreaks, stand conditions largely determine the potential for bark beetle population growth and spread\x20
| (McCambridge and Knight, 1972; Schmid and Frye, 1977;\x20
| Cole and Amman, 1980; Bartos and Amman, 1989; Anhold\x20
| et al., 1996; Hansen et al., 2001; Hindmarch and Reid, 2001;\x20
| Fettig et al., 2007; Hebertson and Jenkins, 2007). Historically,\x20
| forest dynamics and patterns resulting from various fire regimes\x20
| largely influenced the susceptibility of stands to bark beetle\x20

| suppression and exclusion, livestock grazing, and timber\x20
| harvesting have altered forest structure and composition (Arno,\x20
| 1980; Covington et al., 1997). The specific effect of altered\x20
| forest composition and structure on bark beetle epidemiology\x20
| will vary with tree species, the effectiveness of fire suppression,\x20
| historic fire regime and geographic parameters. One conse­
| quence of change in some forest types is that larger and more\x20
| contiguous landscapes have become simultaneously suscep­
| tible to bark beetle outbreaks due to the high percentage of old,\x20
| large diameter, and less vigorous host trees (Covington et al.,\x20
| 1997; Samman and Logan, 2000; Gallant et al., 2003). In\x20
| general, factors that increase susceptible tree numbers and\x20
| decrease heterogeneity increase bark beetle hazard (Amman,\x20
| 1977; Schmid and Frye, 1976; Rudinsky, 1966). After\x20
| reviewing extensive literature, Keane et al. (2002) concluded\x20
| that fire suppression programs have resulted in atypical fuel\x20
| accumulations, declines in forest health, greater risk to\x20
| firefighters and increased likelihood of insect and disease\x20
| epidemics. Sibold and others (2006), however, found little\x20
| evidence to support the assertion that fire suppression practices\x20
| have altered the historic range of variability in their study area\x20
| in Rocky Mountain National Park.\x20
| In the Intermountain West, Douglas-fir beetle (D. pseudot­
| sugae Hopkins), mountain pine beetle (D. ponderosae\x20
| Hopkins), and spruce beetle (D. rufipennis Kirby) are capable\x20
| of causing extensive mortality of host trees in relatively short\x20
| periods of time (Mielke, 1950; Massey and Wygant, 1954;\x20
| Amman, 1977; Schmid and Frye, 1977; Furniss and Orr, 1978;\x20
| McGregor and Cole, 1985; Amman et al., 1988b; Patterson,\x20
| 1991; Schmitz and Gibson, 1996; Holsten et al., 1999; Samman\x20
| and Logan, 2000; Dymerski et al., 2001). This mortality alters\x20
| the amount, composition and arrangement of living and dead\x20
| biomass in various fuel complexes (Schmid and Frye, 1977;\x20
| Cole and Amman, 1980; McGregor and Cole, 1985; Hadley and\x20
| Veblen, 1993; Hadley, 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998; Hopkins,\x20
| 1909; Kulakowski et al., 2003). Stands with a significant loss of\x20
| overstory trees may also experience increased solar input\x20
| resulting in higher temperatures and decreased relative\x20
| humidity desiccating fuels and lowering fuel moisture content\x20
| (Brown, 1975). It has long been assumed that such changes in\x20
| fuel complexes increase the potential for fire occurrence, and\x20
| fire intensity and severity (Brown, 1975; Lotan et al., 1985).\x20
| Policies outlined in the National Fire Plan and the Healthy\x20
| Forest Restoration Act of 2003 assume that bark beetle\x20
| epidemics increase fire hazard for extended periods. It is true\x20
| that any factor that alters the quantity and/or quality of the fuels\x20
| complex potentially affects fire hazard. In reality, little research\x20
| provides specific, quantitative descriptions of bark beetle-
| caused modifications to forest fuels over time. Romme et al.\x20
| (2006) suggested that few scientific studies support the\x20
| assertion that insect outbreaks set the stage for severe forest\x20
| fires.\x20
| The majority of research to date has focused on the\x20
| influences of the mountain pine beetle on fuels accumulation\x20
| and subsequent wildfires in lodgepole pine (Pinus contorta\x20
| Dougl. ex Loud) forests (Brown, 1975; Geiszler et al., 1980; Armour, 1982; Lotan et al., 1985; Romme et al., 1986; Lynch\x20
| et al., 2006). Some research has investigated the spatial and\x20
| temporal relationships of historic spruce beetle outbreaks and\x20
| fires in Colorado (Bebi et al., 2003; Kulakowski et al., 2003;\x20
| Bigler et al., 2005; Kulakowski and Veblen, 2007). Research to\x20
| examine relationships between Douglas-fir beetle-caused tree\x20
| mortality and mixed severity wildfires is very limited.\x20
| Literature reviewed by Parker et al. (2006) presents an\x20
| uncritical acceptance of the hypothesis that fire exclusion is\x20
| the main cause of bark beetle outbreaks. The literature they\x20
| cite supports the contention that outbreaks contribute to a\x20
| buildup of standing and down fuels increasing fire occurrence\x20
| and severity. More empirical research has been done to\x20
| compare the extent of historic bark beetle outbreaks mapped\x20
| using aerial detection surveys. Kulakowski and Veblen (2007),\x20
| for example, used aerial detection survey maps and other\x20
| spatially explicit forest disturbance data to model the effect of\x20
| prior disturbances, including spruce beetle outbreaks, on\x20
| severity of wildfire in Colorado subalpine forests. They found\x20
| no difference in fire severity among stands with current\x20
| outbreaks and endemic stands. Using similar data layers,\x20
| Lynch et al. (2006) found that the odds of burning increased in\x20
| mountain pine beetle-killed stands in one of two outbreaks\x20
| studied following the Yellowstone fires of 1988. No significant\x20
| association was detected in the second mountain pine beetle\x20
| outbreak. Their data set was limited, however, because it did\x20
| not consider the historic stand conditions, and the temporal\x20
| and spatial variability of the beetle-influenced fuels stratum.\x20
| Lynch et al. (2006) indicated that the accuracy of aerial\x20
| detection survey data was more than sufficient for their\x20
| analysis. Kulakowski and Veblen (2007) suggested, however,\x20
| that errors inherent in aerial detection surveys of bark beetle\x20
| activity limit conclusions regarding the influence of bark\x20
| beetle outbreaks on fire extent and severity. They indicated\x20
| that future studies could benefit from methods that incorporate\x20
| ground surveys to better measure the extent of current and past\x20
| outbreak events. These studies are further confounded by\x20
| consideration of factors such as geography, climate and\x20
| disturbance history which make isolation of the cause and\x20
| effect relationship of fire/fuels/beetle factors difficult. Addi­
| tionally, they do not consider other important factors that\x20
| affect the spatial distribution of burned and unburned areas\x20
| such as real time fire weather.\x20

| species. We then use the fuels data to develop and describe\x20

| our data with existing research relating bark beetle-caused tree\x20
| mortality to fuels and potential changes in fire behavior. Finally,\x20

| 2. Disturbance regimes\x20
| The interplay between fuels, weather, and topography\x20
| primarily determines the pattern of fire in vegetation over time,\x20
| commonly called the fire regime (Pyne et al., 1996).\x20
| Topography is constant while fuels and weather can vary\x20
| substantially over time and space. The term ‘fuels’ refers to the\x20
| living and dead biomass that is burnable under certain moisture\x20
| conditions, which varies by forest type, time of year, and\x20
| disturbance history (Brown and Davis, 1973; Albini, 1976;\x20
| Pyne et al., 1996). Fuels are grouped by size classes which\x20
| affect surface area to volume ratios, and the fuels’ ability to\x20
| respond to changing moisture conditions. Smaller fuels are able\x20
| to respond to changes in atmospheric moisture more quickly\x20
| than larger fuels (Anderson, 1985). Weather, in terms of its\x20
| effects on fires, refers to both short-term phenomena such as\x20
| wind speed and relative humidity and long-term trends such as\x20
| drought and flooding (Schroeder and Buck, 1970). Throughout\x20
| the paper, we follow the definitions proposed by Hardy (2005)\x20
| for fire hazard and fire risk and fire severity. He considers fire\x20
| risk as the probability of ignition, fire hazard as the state of the\x20
| fuel, and fire severity as fire effects on wildland systems.\x20
| 2.1. Douglas-fir fire regime\x20
| Intermountain Douglas-fir forests typically occupy steep,\x20
| moist, northern aspects at mid elevations (1850–2500 m)\x20
| between the lower elevation ponderosa pine (Pinus ponderosa\x20
| Dougl. ex Laws) zone and higher elevation, mixed conifer\x20
| forests (Lanner, 1984; Harlow et al., 1979). This mid­
| elevational, vegetative gradient where Douglas-fir occurs has\x20
| a mixed severity fire regime characterized by both short interval\x20
| surface fires and long interval crown fires (Kilgore, 1981;\x20
| Wright and Bailey, 1982). Arno (1976) found that on the\x20
| Bitterroot National Forest surface fire return intervals ranged\x20
| from 15 to 30 years at lower elevation sites to 50 years on moist,\x20
| higher elevation sites. Crown fires occurred at intervals ranging\x20
| between 35 and 60 years (Arno, 1976). This variation in fire\x20
| frequency and intensity primarily depends upon specific site\x20
| conditions such as stand characteristics, habitat type, climate,\x20
| and fuel loads (Arno, 1980). Consequently, Douglas-fir can\x20
| form pure even-aged or uneven-aged stands across a variety of\x20
| habitat types (Mauk and Henderson, 1984). In uneven-aged\x20
| stands, common associates include subalpine and white fir\x20
| (Abies concolor (Gord. and Glend.) Lindl. ex Hildebr.), quaking\x20
| aspen (Populus tremuloides Michx.), ponderosa pine, limber\x20
| pine (Pinus flexilis James), and spruce (Picea sp.). Where fires\x20
| have occurred less frequently, individual Douglas-firs might be\x20
| up to 150 cm at diameter at breast height (dbh) (Lanner, 1984).\x20
| During the interval between stand-replacing fires, Douglas-
| fir stands are commonly subjected to wind, snow avalanches,\x20
| and Douglas-fir beetle infestations among other disturbances.\x20
| The interplay between disturbance agents often creates a\x20
| mosaic of even-aged patches across the landscape. As the age of an individual patch increases, it becomes increasingly\x20
| susceptible to Douglas-fir beetle activity especially where\x20
| recently fallen trees provide suitable habitat for colonization\x20
| and brood production (Rudinsky, 1966; Hebertson and Jenkins,\x20
| 2007). Coincident with the lengthening fire-free interval is an\x20
| increase in understory conifer establishment including Dou­
| glas-fir and true fir. In many Intermountain Douglas-fir stands,\x20
| western spruce budworm, Choristorneura occidentalis Free­
| man (Lepidoptera: Tortricidae), activity ranges from periodic to\x20
| chronic (Wulf and Cates, 1987). Budworm outbreaks are\x20
| exacerbated in multistoried stands and can decrease Douglas-fir\x20
| resistance to bark beetles and contribute to fuel changes\x20
| (Fredricks and Jenkins, 1988). Douglas-fir beetle populations\x20
| can also increase following low-intensity surface fires where\x20
| the insects are more likely to colonize fire-damaged trees\x20
| (Cunningham et al., 2005; Parker et al., 2006). As beetle-caused\x20
| tree mortality increases, the quantity and quality of the fuels\x20
| complex changes, increasing the potential fireline intensity of\x20
| subsequent fires. The increased intensity in older patches will\x20
| perpetuate the age class mosaic into future fire rotations.\x20
| 2.2. Lodgepole pine fire regime\x20
| Lodgepole pine forests in western North America typify fire\x20
| dependent ecosystems (Agee, 1993). These forests become\x20
| increasingly flammable with time and traits including cone\x20
| serotiny, volatile foliage, thin bark, high reproductive capacity,\x20
| and seedling survival evolved in response to fire (Lotan, 1976;\x20
| Schoennagel et al., 2003). With lodgepole pine forests\x20
| occurring at elevations ranging between 2500 and 3000 m,\x20
| however, local fire regimes can vary widely. For example, fire\x20
| return intervals in the northern Rockies may range from 200\x20
| years or more, to as few as 20 years with combinations of low-
| intensity surface fires and high-intensity crown fires (Arno,\x20
| 1980; Lotan et al., 1985; Barrett et al., 1997). Frequent and less\x20
| intense surface fires are more common in lower elevation\x20
| forests characterized by dry summers (Arno, 1980). At higher\x20
| elevations, fire return intervals lengthen and the potential for\x20
| high-intensity crown fires increases due to the cooler and\x20
| moister climate conditions (Arno, 1980; Romme, 1982). In\x20
| northern Utah, fire-free intervals ranged from 12 to 122 years\x20
| during pre-settlement times (Wadleigh and Jenkins, 1996).\x20
| Crown fire development in lodgepole pine stands depends upon\x20
| sufficient heat release from surface fuels, the height of the tree\x20
| crowns above the ground, and current weather conditions\x20
| (Bradley et al., 1992). In turn, these factors are dependent upon\x20
| specific stand conditions, locations, and fuel loadings. Many\x20
| even-aged lodgepole pine stands owe their origin to stand-
| replacing fires (Lotan et al., 1985).\x20
| Following stand-replacing fires, lodgepole pine quickly\x20
| colonizes burned sites (Lotan, 1976). After approximately 20\x20
| years, subalpine fir begins to establish beneath lodgepole pine.\x20
| During this period of stand development, the fuels complex in\x20
| some lodgepole pine stands is conducive to low-intensity\x20
| surface fire (Jenkins et al., 1998). The occurrence and influence\x20
| of surface fire on the lodgepole pine fire regime varies.\x20
| Wadleigh and Jenkins (1996) reported that surface fire was\x20
text: sorted page 5
| 20 M.J. Jenkins et al. / Forest Ecology and Management 254 (2008) 16–34 common during the stand-replacing fire interval in the central\x20
| Rocky Mountains. Jenkins et al. (1998) evaluated the influence\x20
| of surface fire on successional patterns in subalpine spruce-fir\x20
| forests in northern Utah. Sibold and others (2006), however,\x20
| reported that surface fire affected only 1–3% of the forested\x20

| surface fire kills late-successional species, such as subalpine fir,\x20
| with little damage to lodgepole pine. In the absence of fire,\x20
| several more years are required for lodgepole pine stands to\x20
| reach peak fire susceptibility (Brown, 1975). Stand-replacing\x20
| fires become possible due to the lack of self-pruning, standing\x20
| snags, and the presence of shade tolerant species enhancing\x20
| vertical continuity. After about 60 years, lodgepole pine begins\x20
| to self-prune and snags fall creating stands that are moderately\x20
| fire resistant (Brown, 1975). Lodgepole pine stands of this age,\x20
| however, become increasingly susceptible to mountain pine\x20
| beetle infestation, particularly at mid-elevations (Amman,\x20
| 1977). Geiszler et al. (1980) found that mountain pine beetles\x20
| tend to initially colonize trees damaged by low-intensity\x20
| surface fires. Fire-damaged tissue also provides entry courts for\x20
| decay fungi that gradually weaken the tree resulting in downfall\x20
| and canopy openings (Gara, 1988). Lodgepole pine seedlings,\x20
| understory shrubs and herbaceous plants develop in openings\x20
| (Cole and Amman, 1980). With susceptible stand conditions,\x20
| populations of mountain pine beetles can reach outbreak levels\x20
| causing extensive tree mortality (Gara et al., 1985). Conse­
| quently, outbreaks in association with fire serve to maintain\x20
| host stands in a younger, more vigorous seral state (Geiszler\x20
| et al., 1980; Gara et al., 1985; Baker and Veblen, 1990; Veblen\x20
| et al., 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998). Where lodgepole pine is\x20
| persistent, or in the absence of fire, mountain pine beetle\x20
| outbreaks create uneven-aged stand conditions by removing\x20
| pockets of larger trees. Shrubs and herbaceous species invade\x20
| the openings, but over time, diminish as lodgepole pine saplings\x20
| assume dominance. New gaps seeded in by lodgepole pine\x20
| result in different age classes of trees and multistoried stands\x20
| (Cole and Amman, 1980). Without fire, and depending on site\x20
| conditions, major reductions in lodgepole pine allow for the\x20
| release of understory Engelmann spruce and subalpine fir at\x20
| higher elevations and convert the stand to Douglas-fir at lower\x20
| elevations (Roe and Amman, 1970; Bradley et al., 1992;\x20
| Jenkins et al., 1998).\x20
| 2.3. Engelmann spruce/subalpine fir fire regime\x20
| A high-severity fire regime typically characterizes Inter­
| mountain spruce-fir forests (Agee, 1993; Edmonds et al., 2000).\x20
| Elevations exceed 2400 m in the spruce-fir zone and climates\x20
| are generally cool and moist in summer (Alexander, 1987).\x20
| These conditions primarily influence the flammability of fine\x20
| fuels reducing ignition risk and initial fire spread. Fire weather\x20
| conditions conducive to extreme fire behavior are also limited\x20
| to a few weeks in late summer during most years. As a\x20
| consequence, the frequency of wildfires in spruce-fir forests is\x20
| low with return intervals often greater than 100 years (Schmid\x20
| and Frye, 1977; Wright and Bailey, 1982; Alexander, 1987;\x20
| Baker and Veblen, 1990; Veblen et al., 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998). For example, Jenkins et al. (1998) determined that 200–\x20
| 400 years are needed for a spruce-fir climax forest to develop in\x20
| northern Utah, which generally agrees with other fire history\x20
| studies in the northern Rockies (Arno, 1980). Environments for\x20
| tree establishment within the spruce-fir zone are marginal\x20
| (Daniel and Schmidt, 1972; Paden et al., 1978; Knapp and\x20
| Smith, 1982; Alexander, 1987). Survival is especially low on\x20
| burned sites where extreme heat, frost, and browsing kill\x20
| unprotected Engelmann spruce seedlings. Consequently,\x20
| grasses and shrubs may dominate post-fire communities for\x20
| more than a century (Edmonds et al., 2000).\x20
| The fuels complex that develops in spruce-fir forests between\x20
| major wildfires creates the potential for crown fires rather than\x20
| surface fires (Taylor and Fonda, 1990). Trees with tightly\x20
| arranged crowns and low growing branches that are often\x20
| covered with lichens facilitate crown fire spread. Under dense,\x20
| closed canopy conditions, spruce-fir stands have, on average\x20
| sparse understories and a compact litter layer comprised of short,\x20
| fine needles that limit the potential for low-intensity surface fires\x20
| (Bradley et al., 1992; Swetnam and Baisan, 1996). Therefore,\x20
| many even-aged spruce-fir forests likely resulted from stand-
| replacing fires (Loope and Gruell, 1973).\x20
| During the relatively long period between major fire events,\x20
| spruce beetle-caused tree mortality has an important influence\x20
| on the vegetative dynamics and patterns of spruce-fir forests.\x20
| Following severe outbreaks, the return to climax spruce-
| dominated communities may require up to several hundred\x20
| years (Schmid and Frye, 1977; Baker and Veblen, 1990; Veblen\x20
| et al., 1991a; Veblen et al., 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998). With the\x20
| death of overstory spruce, advanced regeneration releases. The\x20
| majority of advanced regeneration, particularly on dry sites at\x20
| lower elevations is subalpine fir. The relatively small proportion\x20
| of advanced spruce regeneration in the understory of mature\x20
| stands can be attributed to the inability of spruce seedlings to\x20
| establish in heavy duff layers and several species of fungi that\x20
| specifically infect spruce seeds (Daniel and Schmidt, 1972;\x20
| Paden et al., 1978; Knapp and Smith, 1982; Alexander, 1987;\x20
| Aplet et al., 1988). Consequently, subalpine-fir typically\x20
| dominates stands for about 200 years until trees succumb to\x20
| the effects of disease (Veblen, 1986a,b; Aplet et al., 1988).\x20
| Dead and windthrown subalpine fir create canopy gaps and\x20
| upturned mineral soil that provide suitable habitat for spruce\x20
| seedling establishment (Shea, 1985; Aplet et al., 1988). Several\x20
| more decades may elapse before mature spruce dominates the\x20
| main canopy once again. Veblen et al. (1994) reported that\x20
| spruce-fir stands affected by the 1940s outbreak on the White\x20
| River Plateau, Colorado had a scarcity of spruce older than 140\x20
| years.\x20
| 3. Bark beetle ecology\x20
| 3.1. Douglas-fir beetle\x20
| The Douglas-fir beetle occurs throughout the range of its\x20
| primary host, Douglas-fir (Schmitz and Gibson, 1996).\x20
| Endemic populations of beetles typically infest downed host\x20
| material and large diameter trees weakened by drought, fire,\x20
text: sorted page 6
| M.J. Jenkins et al. / Forest Ecology and Management 254 (2008) 16–34 21 defoliation, and root diseases (Furniss, 1965; Coulson and\x20
| Witter, 1984; Christiansen et al., 1987; Fredricks and Jenkins,\x20
| 1988; Jenkins, 1990; Stark, 1993; Cunningham et al., 2005).\x20
| Populations have been known to reach outbreak levels\x20
| following disturbances that produce an abundance of green\x20
| host material (Lejeune et al., 1961; Hadley and Veblen, 1993).\x20
| Outbreaks also appear to coincide with prolonged periods of\x20
| drought.\x20
| Hadley (1994) found that the vulnerability of post-fire stands\x20
| to bark beetle attack was related to both aspect and topography.\x20
| Specifically, it appears that stands of Douglas-fir on north-
| facing slopes are more susceptible to beetle attack. This may be\x20
| due to the effects of fire suppression resulting in higher tree\x20
| densities and a more suitable distribution of large diameter trees\x20
| (Hadley and Veblen, 1993; Hadley, 1994). In the Intermountain\x20
| region, Douglas-fir beetle outbreaks are site specific depending\x20
| on the availability of susceptible hosts and mortality centers\x20
| may be confined to small groups of trees. Within highly\x20
| susceptible stands or landscapes, beetles may kill large groups\x20
| of trees (exceeding 100 trees) and result in 60–80% mortality of\x20
| the Douglas-fir over 30 cm in diameter (Furniss and Orr, 1978;\x20
| Amman and Ryan, 1991; Patterson, 1991; Schmitz and Gibson,\x20
| 1996). Larger diameter trees have thicker phloem necessary for\x20
| greater reproductive success (Lessard and Schmid, 1990;\x20
| Amman and Ryan, 1991). Bark beetles generally only attack\x20
| smaller diameter trees during epidemics.\x20
| Stands considered ‘‘high hazard’’ for a Douglas-fir beetle\x20
| outbreak have average Douglas-fir diameters greater than 23 cm\x20
| dbh, average age of Douglas-fir in the stand greater than 120\x20
| years, stocking densities exceeding 28 m2/ha, and more than a\x20
| 50% Douglas-fir component. Outbreaks of Douglas-fir beetles\x20
| typically last 2–4 years, but periods of unusually dry weather\x20
| (Schmitz and Gibson, 1996) or budworm defoliation (Fredericks\x20
| and Jenkins, 1988) may prolong activity in certain areas.\x20
| 3.2. Mountain pine beetle\x20
| The mountain pine beetle is the most aggressive bark beetle\x20
| in western North America. In the Intermountain West, this\x20
| insect primarily infests and kills lodgepole pine, ponderosa\x20
| pine, white bark pine (Pinus albicaulis Engelm.), limber pine,\x20
| and other western white pines throughout their range. During\x20
| severe outbreaks, mountain pine beetles may also attack\x20
| Douglas-fir, true firs, and spruce, although successful brood\x20
| production in these species is rare (Furniss and Carolin, 1980).\x20
| When populations are low, beetles infest weakened and injured\x20
| trees. Drought stress often contributes to the initiation of\x20
| mountain pine beetle outbreaks (Mattson and Haack, 1987;\x20
| Goheen and Hansen, 1993). Mountain pine beetle spread occurs\x20
| most rapidly in old, dense stands comprised of a majority of\x20
| large lodgepole pines. The most susceptible stands have trees\x20
| over 80 years old, average tree diameters greater than 20 cm\x20
| dbh, an average phloem thickness greater than 0.254 cm, over\x20
| 18 m2/ha mean basal area, and stand density index between 140\x20
| and 245 (Cole and Amman, 1980; Berryman, 1982; Amman\x20
| et al., 1985; McGregor and Cole, 1985; Safranyik, 1989;\x20
| Anhold et al., 1996). During outbreaks, beetles infest the older, large diameter trees first, and eventually kill smaller trees as\x20
| populations build (McGregor and Cole, 1985). Between 1998\x20
| and 2005, mountain pine beetles killed approximately 27\x20
| million lodgepole over 149,734 ha in the Sawtooth National\x20
| Recreation Area, Idaho (USDA FS, 2006). Epidemics usually\x20
| collapse with the loss of all or most large trees.\x20
| 3.3. Spruce beetle\x20
| The spruce beetle is the most important biotic disturbance\x20
| agent affecting post-fire, spruce-fir forests in the Intermountain\x20
| West (Veblen et al., 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998). Engelmann\x20
| spruce serves as the principle host for spruce beetles, although\x20
| they may attack Colorado blue spruce (Picea pungens Engelm.)\x20
| and lodgepole pine during epidemics (Miller, 1970; Schmid and\x20
| Frye, 1977). Endemic populations of spruce beetles typically\x20
| reproduce within newly fallen trees (Schmid, 1981). Occa­
| sionally they will also attack large, overmature spruce, killing\x20
| trees individually or in small groups creating canopy gaps\x20
| (Veblen et al., 1991b; Goyer et al., 1998). Local populations can\x20
| build to epidemic levels following stand disturbances that\x20
| produce an abundance of suitable host material (Schmid and\x20
| Frye, 1977; Jenkins et al., 1998; Hebertson and Jenkins, 2007).\x20
| As competition for food intensifies, beetles may then attack\x20
| standing live trees. Stand conditions, host vigor, climate, and\x20
| existing population levels also influence the initiation of\x20
| outbreaks and the spread of spruce beetles through living stands\x20
| (Schmid and Frye, 1976; Schmid, 1981; Hard, 1985; Werner\x20
| and Holsten, 1985; Paine et al., 1997; Jenkins et al., 1998;\x20
| Holsten et al., 1999; Hansen et al., 2001; Hebertson and\x20
| Jenkins, 2007). Dense stands comprised of over 65% large\x20
| diameter spruce (greater than 40 cm dbh) are most susceptible\x20
| to spruce beetle attack. Recent spruce beetle outbreaks in Utah\x20
| have resulted in tree mortality exceeding 1.5 million spruce\x20
| trees and the loss of over 90% of the large spruce component\x20
| (Dymerski et al., 2001; Keyes et al., 2003; USDA FS, 2006).\x20

| 5. Fire behavior in bark beetle-affected conifer fuels\x20
| 5.1. Fire behavior\x20

| elevations in the subalpine zone (Bessie and Johnson, 1995).\x20

| 5.5. Crown fire relationships\x20
| The potential transition from a surface fire to a passive\x20
| crown is determined by a critical intensity of the surface fire and\x20
| the crown base height that provides a vertically continuous fuel\x20
| stratum of sufficiently low fuel moisture. This phenomenon is\x20
| commonly called torching in ladder fuels. The potential\x20
| transition from passive to active crown fire is determined by\x20
| canopy closure or canopy bulk density, foliar moisture content,\x20
| and effective wind speed. Rothermel (1991) developed models\x20
| that defined the above variables and used foliar moisture inputs\x20
| from Van Wagner (1977) to predict crown fire initiation and\x20
| spread. We applied the crown fire spread model to the fuels data\x20
| to predict crown fire development and spread.\x20
| We found dead aerial fuels in the current epidemic stands\x20
| will increase the likelihood of surface fires transitioning into\x20
| canopy fuels for a given surface fire intensity more than in\x20
| endemic stands. Current fire prediction models, however, are\x20
| based only on live fuels and do not incorporate the effect of\x20
| dead aerial fuels in the overstory, which can comprise as much\x20
| as 30% of the total foliage in current epidemic stands (Page and\x20
| Jenkins, 2007a). Differences in chemical composition of live\x20
| versus dead foliage, such as volatile oils, and flammability may\x20
| also influence crown fire spread (Van Wagner, 1974). The\x20
| crown fire model utilizes live foliar moisture content, greater\x20
| than 70% of oven dry weight, and crown base height to model\x20
| crown fire initiation. However, fuel moistures for dead fuels are\x20
| generally less than 30% of oven dry weight and may be much\x20
| lower depending upon specific environmental conditions. Thus,\x20
| the model we used may be inadequate to predict crown fire\x20
| potential in the current epidemic stands where a range of live,\x20
| dead and dying foliage contribute to crown fire initiation and\x20
| spread. Nevertheless, several studies have demonstrated that\x20
| dead needles with moisture contents as low as 7.7% of oven dry\x20
| weight will ignite with significantly lower temperatures\x20
| compared to live fuels, increasing the potential for passive\x20
| crown fire development (Stockstad, 1975; Xanthopoulos and\x20
| Wakimoto, 1993).\x20

| 5.6. Ecological effects of extreme fire behavior\x20
| The timing of high-intensity crown fires may influence post-

| Despain (1990), however, put forth the hypothesis that\x20
| mountain pine beetle outbreaks actually decrease the risk of\x20

| inhibited. He noted examples in Yellowstone National Park\x20

| outbreaks, although admitted that his observations were merely\x20

| 6. Bark beetle management strategies and implications\x20
| for fuels and fire behavior\x20
| Land managers often employ various prevention, suppres­
| sion and restoration strategies to mitigate potentially adverse\x20
| impacts resulting from bark beetle outbreaks. Prevention\x20
| strategies often utilize silvicultural practices including thinning\x20
| to modify stand conditions favorable to insect and disease\x20
| agents and should occur before populations reach unmanage­
| able levels. After the initiation of an outbreak, management\x20
| options become increasingly limited and delays in treatment\x20
| greatly reduce opportunities for success (Fettig et al., 2007). By\x20
| enhancing stand diversity and resiliency, prevention strategies\x20
| can help avoid unacceptable losses of valuable resources,\x20
| maintain or enhance resource objectives, and maximize\x20
| revenue in the long term (Schmid and Frye, 1977; Cahill,\x20
| 1978; Cole et al., 1983; McGregor et al., 1987; Amman et al.,\x20
| 1988a,b; Fettig et al., 2007; Schowalter et al., 1992; Anhold\x20
| et al., 1996; Schmitz and Gibson, 1996; Holsten et al., 1999).\x20
| Suppression strategies are usually implemented with the\x20
| detection of building bark beetle populations. These strategies\x20
| include sanitation and salvage treatments, insecticide applica­
| tions and the use of pheromones to reduce local beetle\x20
| populations and protect high value trees from bark beetle attack\x20
| in recreation sites. When implemented during the initial stages\x20
| of an outbreak, suppression activities can reduce population\x20
| levels and the distribution and extent of bark beetle-caused tree\x20
| mortality. Treatments associated with suppression alternatives,\x20
| however, are usually limited, often occurring at small scales.\x20
| Because treatment alternatives do not modify stand conditions\x20
| at large scales, the benefits are often short term. Restoration\x20
| activities attempt to reestablish vegetation and promote the\x20
| long-term resiliency of forests to insect and disease outbreaks\x20
| or attacks.\x20
| Reducing the accumulation of hazardous fuels and/or the\x20
| potential for extreme fire behavior resulting from bark beetle\x20
| mortality is often cited as a rationale for implementing bark\x20
| beetle management treatments (Schott, 1994; Gorte, 1996;\x20
| USDA Forest Service, 1998). Romme et al. (2006) suggested\x20
| that there is little ecological justification in removing insect-
| killed trees. Additionally, slash produced during bark beetle\x20
| treatments and post-harvest blow down will result in fuel\x20
| complexes different from those in either bark beetle-killed or\x20
| uninfested stands. Conventional slash fuel models (Rothermel,\x20
| 1983) used to estimate fire behavior in stands treated for bark\x20
| beetles suggest increased potential for ignition and alteration\x20
| of fire behavior depending on the nature of the treatment\x20
| (Turner et al., 1994; DellaSala et al., 1995).\x20
| To determine how fuels might differ in spruce-fir forests\x20
| managed for spruce beetle versus unmanaged forests, we\x20
| measured fuels in thinned and control stands on the Fishlake\x20
| National Forest in south central Utah (Jenkins et al., 2005).\x20
| Moderately thinned stands had residual basal areas ranging between 23 and 28.4 m2/ha with average spruce diameters of\x20
| 30.5 cm dbh. Heavily thinned stands had residual basal areas of\x20
| 13.8–18.3 m2/ha with average spruce diameters of 25.4 cm dbh.\x20
| Fuels measurements were then used to create custom fuel\x20
| models to compare potential fire behavior among the\x20
| treatments. Fine fuels (1, 10, and 100 h size classes) increased\x20
| in treated stands with the greatest amount of total fuel\x20
| accumulating in the heavily thinned plots. Moderately thinned\x20
| plots generally had the lowest fuel depths. Heavily thinned\x20
| plots had a greater average fuel depth compared to the control\x20
| plot in some stands. The 1000 h fuels were quite variable from\x20
| treatment to treatment in the areas sampled. As expected, the\x20
| amount of available crown fuel significantly dropped in each of\x20
| the thinning treatments. Crown bulk densities were also\x20
| significantly lower for the thinning treatments.\x20
| Predicted fire behavior was greatest in heavily thinned\x20
| stands where the increase in fuel depth, the amount of fuel,\x20
| along with a higher mid-flame wind speed increased potential\x20
| fire behavior. In other areas, the greatest fire behavior was in the\x20
| control plots associated with the highest wind speed. Although\x20
| more fuel was available in the heavily thinned stands and the\x20
| mid-flame wind speed was greater, the fuel depth was less than\x20
| that of the control. This was due to the mechanically compacted\x20
| fuel bed which does not support combustion as well as the more\x20

| noted that fire behavior prediction system, BEHAVE plus, is\x20

| result.\x20
| Fire behavior in the heavily thinned plots, however,\x20
| was probably under-predicted because the fuel moistures used\x20
| did not take into account the effect of increased solar radia­
| tion reaching the exposed fuel bed. When compared to the\x20
| heavily thinned and the control for both areas, the moderately\x20
| thinned plots had intermediate fire behavior characteristics. In\x20
| terms of crown fires, the control plots had the greatest potential\x20
| to sustain a crown fire of some kind (i.e. lowest crown fire\x20
| index).\x20

| behavior resulting from spruce beetle management strategies\x20

| thinning treatments and influences on fire behavior in western\x20

| Skinner, 2005). Moderate thinnings may result in less potential\x20
| for extreme behavior compared to unmanaged stands. Greater\x20
| fuel depths, mid-flame wind speeds and lower fuel moistures in\x20
| heavily treated stands (>60% basal area reduction) might\x20
| increase potential fire behavior compared to unmanaged stands.\x20

| thoroughly consider post-harvest fuel treatments to reduce\x20
| surface fuels. Thinning followed by sufficient treatment of\x20
| surface fuels usually outweighs changes in fire weather factors\x20
| (wind speed and fuel moisture) resulting in an overall reduction\x20
| in expected fire behavior (Weatherspoon, 1996). Due to\x20
| similarities in bark beetle-created fuels, this consideration\x20

| bark beetle species as well.\x20

| 7.1. Fuels management strategies\x20
| With growing concern over the occurrence of uncharacter­
| istic wildfires in the western United States, federal and state\x20
| land management agencies have initiated aggressive fuels\x20

| practices often include prescribed fire or mechanical treatments\x20

| these treatments, however, can injure residual trees predispos­

| Rippy et al., 2005; Arno, 2000; McCullough et al., 1998).\x20
| Increased amounts of host material (slash and stumps) and host\x20
| volatiles in treated stands can also attract bark beetles (Schmid,\x20
| 1977; Amman et al., 1988b; Six et al., 2002; Fettig et al., 2006).\x20

| adult beetle flight periods, (2) remove slash and all susceptible\x20

| site using pile-and-burn, lop-and-scatter, or chipping methods,\x20
| (3) remove or burn any infested host material, (4) limit the\x20
| diameter and length of host material, and (5) avoid thinning in\x20
| stands highly susceptible to root diseases (Six et al., 2002;\x20
| Steed and Wagner, 2004; Rippy et al., 2005; Fettig et al., 2006).\x20

| et al., 2006). Where chipping is the preferable treatment\x20
| method, Fettig et al. (2006) suggested allowing slash to dry\x20
| before treatment and limiting the amount of chips around the\x20
| bases of residual trees. With the inability to treat slash using one\x20
| of the above methods, funnel traps baited with aggregation\x20
| pheromones and placed around piles can capture a significant\x20
| number of emerging adult beetles (Ross and Daterman, 1997).\x20
| Regardless of treatment, it is important for land managers to\x20
| assess general beetle population levels and beetle-caused tree\x20
| mortality prior to treatment and monitor treated areas regularly.\x20
| 7.2. Delayed tree mortality following fire\x20
| Less obvious is the fact that fire can wound trees and render\x20
| them susceptible to bark beetles that in turn can kill trees\x20
| altering the quantity and quality of the fuels complex. Bark\x20
| beetle outbreaks following fires are not unprecedented, but\x20
| neither are they certain. Beetle outbreaks following fires have\x20
| been documented in the Intermountain West. Douglas-fir\x20
| beetle, spruce beetle, and pine engraver beetle (Ips pini Say\x20

| which others may be more susceptible to fungal infections and\x20

| attack fire-injured trees although their direct effect on tree\x20

| Following the Greater Yellowstone fires in 1988 for example,\x20

| the majority of delayed conifer mortality, although bark beetles\x20

| attraction to fire-damaged lodgepole pine (Ryan and Amman,\x20

| landscapes requiring treatment, develop ecologically appro­
| priate and feasible treatment alternatives, decide where and\x20
| when treatments would be most efficacious and over what\x20
| amount of time (Agee and Skinner, 2005). Multiple resource\x20
| objectives often necessitate that management strategies\x20
| prioritize landscapes for treatment. Land managers should\x20
| not consider every landscape with hazardous bark beetle or\x20
| fuels conditions a high priority candidate for treatment\x20
| (Schoennagel et al., 2004; Agee and Skinner, 2005). For\x20
| example, lower elevation drier forest types might receive higher\x20
| priority for large-scale treatments than higher elevation forest\x20
| types (Agee and Skinner, 2005). The immediate implementa­
| tion of large-scale treatments in wildland/urban interfaces,\x20
| however, may be appropriate in all forest types (Glitzenstein\x20
| et al., 2006). Other treatment priorities may include landscapes\x20
| with high economic investment, commercial or recreation\x20
| values, or critical wildlife habitat. Prioritization should finally\x20
| consider landscapes where treatments can best enhance long-
| term sustainability and resiliency to change (Agee and Skinner,\x20
| 2005).\x20
| The mitigation of potentially adverse bark beetles and fire\x20
| effects is maximized when treatments occur at landscape scales\x20
| and integrate the spatial arrangement of forest types, stand\x20
| conditions, treatment units and prescriptions (Graham et al.,\x20
| 2004; Agee and Skinner, 2005; Fettig et al., 2007). Small-scale,\x20
| scattered treatments do not effectively reduce the long-term\x20
| susceptibility of forests to bark beetle infestation or fragment\x20
| the landscape fuels (Samman and Logan, 2000; Graham et al.,\x20
| 2004; Agee and Skinner, 2005; Fettig et al., 2007). Failure to\x20
| reduce susceptibility across the landscape and/or address the\x20
| entire bark beetle populations often results in insect spread\x20
| from untreated stands into treated areas with the depletion of\x20
| host resources (Fettig et al., 2007). With extreme fire weather\x20
| conditions, intense fires can also overwhelm small-scale\x20
| treatments (Agee and Skinner, 2005). Even with a reduction\x20
| in fire behavior, treatments with substantial edge adjacent to\x20
| untreated units are likely to suffer substantial tree mortality\x20
| (Agee and Skinner, 2005).\x20
| 9. Future research direction\x20
| Recent and on-going research has provided greater insight\x20
| into the nature of bark beetle mortality, associated fuels\x20
| accumulation, and fire interactions in Intermountain Douglas-
| fir, lodgepole pine and Engelmann spruce forest types. Similar\x20
| research is needed to elucidate these relationships for these\x20
| species in other regions, as well as in western ponderosa pine\x20
| forest types and pinyon-juniper woodlands.\x20
| Many effects of bark beetle-caused injury and mortality on\x20
| fuels and fire behavior remain uncertain. Adult bark beetles and\x20
| larval feeding in conjunction with the introduction and\x20
| colonization of blue-staining fungi (Leptographium spp.)\x20
| disrupt the transport of water to the foliage of host trees\x20
| (Cates and Alexander, 1982). Foliar discoloration indicative of\x20
| needle death typically becomes apparent 1 year after bark\x20
text: sorted page 14
| M.J. Jenkins et al. / Forest Ecology and Management 254 (2008) 16–34 29 beetle attack. Dead needles have low moisture content and\x20
| strongly influence the potential for crown fire initiation and\x20
| rates of crown fire spread. Little is known about the moisture\x20
| content of green needles on current mass attacked trees and how\x20
| initial changes in moisture content might contribute to crown\x20
| fire potential and intensity.\x20
| Custom fuel models used to predict fire behavior in bark\x20
| beetle-affected landscapes indicate that fire severity increases\x20
| as snags begin to fall. Limited research suggests that fall rates\x20
| of bark beetle-killed trees are highly variable or largely\x20
| unknown for many Intermountain forest types. Reliable\x20
| estimates of fall rates also require further study to determine\x20
| the influence of soil and site characteristics, wind, and root\x20
| diseases.\x20
| Current models of bark beetle-affected fuels assume\x20
| homogeneous tree mortality across the landscape. In reality,\x20
| patterns (spatial arrangement and distribution) of bark beetle\x20
| tree mortality and fuels accumulations exhibit considerable\x20
| variability across the landscape depending on existing stand\x20
| conditions, local bark beetle population levels, site character­
| istics, and the distribution of decay, and root disease centers\x20
| (Cobb, 1989; Lundquist, 2007). The development of fire spread\x20
| models in various bark beetle-affected landscapes may help\x20
| land managers determine how patterns of bark beetle mortality\x20
| may decrease or exacerbate (prolong) hazardous fuels (fire\x20
| hazards) and consequently facilitate the identification of stands\x20
| to prioritize for treatment.\x20
| There are currently several new modeling tools including\x20
| Landscape Fire and Resource Management Planning Tools\x20
| Project (LANDFIRE) and the Fire and Fuels Extension to the\x20
| Forest Vegetation Simulator (FFE-FVS) available to fire and\x20
| fuels managers to aid in fuel treatment placement and\x20
| verification. Due to the nature of the data acquisition techniques\x20
| in these programs, there is often inconsistency between the\x20
| actual fuel conditions and conventional default fuel conditions\x20
| provided by the programs (Anderson, 1982; Johnson and\x20
| Peterson, 2005; Scott and Burgan, 2005). The use of site-
| specific fuels data inputs improves the reliability of modeled\x20
| (fire behavior) projections, however the collection of these data\x20
| is often costly and labor intensive. The development of tools\x20
| including photo appraisal guides for bark beetle-affected fuels\x20
| would facilitate collecting large amounts of actual surface fuels\x20
| data for modeling purposes.\x20
| 10. Conclusions\x20
| Bark beetles and their effects on fuel accumulations and\x20
| subsequent fire hazard are poorly understood. Clearly, bark\x20
| beetles cause tree damage and/or mortality that can alter fuels\x20
| complexes of lodgepole pine, Douglas-fir and Engelmann\x20
| spruce forest types, among others, and consequently associated\x20
| fire behavior. Mortality caused by Douglas-fir beetle, mountain\x20
| pine beetle and spruce beetle species can contribute significant\x20
| amounts of large woody debris to the fuels complex (Lotan\x20
| et al., 1985; Romme et al., 1986; McCullough et al., 1998).\x20
| However, a period of 20 or more years after the outbreak may be\x20
| necessary before the majority of beetle-killed trees are on the forest floor (Mielke, 1950). Initially after the outbreak, there are\x20
| substantial increases in the amount of fine dead fuels in the\x20
| canopy. The majority of these fuels remain in the canopy for 2–\x20
| 3 years after the outbreak (Knight, 1987; Schmid and Amman,\x20
| 1992; Page and Jenkins, 2007a). Immediately following the\x20
| outbreak, there may be a large increase in the amount of\x20
| understory shrub and herbaceous plants (Reid, 1989; Stone and\x20
| Wolfe, 1996; Page and Jenkins, 2007a). With an abundance of\x20
| green understory shrub and herbaceous plants there may be a\x20
| decrease in potential surface fire behavior (Stocks, 1987). The\x20
| large amount of fuels present due to beetle mortality may not\x20
| represent abnormal fuel conditions in high elevation systems\x20
| (Brown, 1983; Johnson et al., 2001; Schoennagel et al., 2004).\x20
| In the case of spruce beetle, some research indicates that fuel\x20
| accumulations caused by the spruce beetle do not increase the\x20
| density of fires in a given area when compared to unaffected\x20
| stands (Bebi et al., 2003; Kulakowski et al., 2003). Over\x20
| decades, when there is a significant component of large down\x20
| wood, there will be an increase in fire severity during high-
| intensity fire events.\x20
| Although bark beetles have significant effects on fuels,\x20
| weather conditions play an important role in fires in subalpine\x20
| forests (Bessie and Johnson, 1995). Conifer forests experien­
| cing extreme fire weather are subject to intense fires under\x20
| many fuel conditions, while stands with high fuel loadings will\x20
| only have an affect on crown fire initiation under moderate fire\x20
| weather.\x20
| Depending on pre-existing stand conditions bark beetle\x20
| treatments such as moderate thinnings may result in less\x20
| potential for extreme behavior compared to unmanaged stands.\x20
| Greater fuel depths, mid-flame wind speeds and lower fuel\x20
| moistures in heavily treated stands might increase potential fire\x20
| behavior compared to unmanaged stands. This necessitates\x20
| bark beetle management strategies more thoroughly consider\x20
| post-harvest fuel treatments to reduce surface fuels to lower the\x20
| risk of severe wildfire.\x20
| Fuels reduction treatments may result in residual tree\x20
| damage and/or the production of host material (green slash and\x20
| stumps) and volatiles that can attract and concentrate certain\x20
| bark beetle species, particularly if treatments coincide with\x20
| beetle flight periods. Land managers can mitigate for bark\x20
| beetle population increases by avoiding treatments during bark\x20
| beetle flight, minimizing activities in those portions of stands\x20
| where decay and root diseases are prevalent, and managing\x20
| green slash.\x20
| Bark beetles frequently attack fire-injured trees and\x20
| contribute to delayed tree mortality. Several models have been\x20
| developed that provide land managers with the best predictors\x20
| for the post-fire Douglas-fir mortality. Whether bark beetle\x20
| populations build in fire-damaged trees and contribute to\x20
| outbreaks remains uncertain. Land managers may determine\x20
| the likelihood of bark beetle depredations using assessments of\x20
| stand conditions integrated with burn intensities for bark beetle\x20
| species of concern.\x20
| Management plans that consider bark beetle/fire interactions\x20
| can provide decision-makers with better guidance for meeting\x20
| important resource objectives, reducing treatment costs,\x20
text: sorted page 15
| 30 M.J. Jenkins et al. / Forest Ecology and Management 254 (2008) 16–34 minimizing adverse ecological impacts, and avoiding potential\x20
| controversy. Management plans that explicitly define both short\x20
| and long-term resource objectives enable land managers to\x20
| devise the most appropriate treatment strategies. The mitigation\x20
| of potentially adverse bark beetle and fire effects is maximized\x20
| when treatments occur at landscape scales and integrate the\x20
| spatial arrangement of forest types and stand conditions.\x20

| disturbance in Intermountain Forests over varying temporal and spatial scales. 1. Introduction\x20
| The interactions among disturbances operating over various\x20
| spatial and temporal scales determine the nature of forested\x20
| landscapes. In some western forest systems, for example fire-
| dependent lodgepole pine forests, the influence of a single\x20
| disturbance agent, such as wildfire, is so pervasive that it\x20
| dominates forest dynamics. In most low to mid-elevation\x20
| ponderosa pine forests, the relatively rapid accumulation of\x20
| fuels and weather conditions required for fire ignition and\x20
| spread make seasonal fires common. Fire return intervals from\x20
| as few as 5 up to 100 or more years are capable of driving forest\x20
| succession (Jenkins et al., 1998). In high elevation, subalpine\x20
| forests, lightning is relatively common and sufficient amounts\x20
| of fuels accumulate to sustain fire. However, short snow-free\x20
| periods, low temperatures, and high relative humidities often\x20
| preclude fire occurrence for extended periods (Arno, 2000). In\x20
| these forest systems, Dendroctonus bark beetles (Coleoptera:\x20
| Curculionidae, Scolytinae) coupled with other widespread\x20
| disturbance events such as harvesting, snow avalanches and\x20
| wind throw primarily regulate forest dynamics (Veblen et al.,\x20
| 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998). A conceptual model of the\x20
| interactions of agents of disturbance over large spatial and long\x20
| temporal scales is presented in Fig. 1.\x20
| Bark beetles naturally occur throughout the ranges of their\x20
| host trees and have a critical role within healthy, functioning\x20
| ecosystems (Roe and Amman, 1970; Geiszler et al., 1980; Gara\x20
| et al., 1985; Veblen et al., 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998; Bebi et al.,\x20
| 2003; Kulakowski et al., 2003). During endemic population\x20
| phases, bark beetles typically infest blowdown and trees\x20
| weakened by fire, diseases, and drought (Schmid, 1981; Raffa\x20
| and Berryman, 1983; Mattson and Haack, 1987; Goheen and\x20
| Hansen, 1993; Cunningham et al., 2005). Periodically, popula­
| tions of bark beetles reach outbreak levels infesting otherwise\x20
| healthy trees (Raffa, 2001; Samman and Logan, 2000).\x20
| The level of bark beetle activity fluctuates with the\x20
| availability of suitable host material, stand conditions,\x20
| environmental factors, and the abundance of parasites and\x20
| predators. Although many factors may contribute to the\x20
| initiation of outbreaks, stand conditions largely determine the potential for bark beetle population growth and spread\x20
| (McCambridge and Knight, 1972; Schmid and Frye, 1977;\x20
| Cole and Amman, 1980; Bartos and Amman, 1989; Anhold\x20
| et al., 1996; Hansen et al., 2001; Hindmarch and Reid, 2001;\x20
| Fettig et al., 2007; Hebertson and Jenkins, 2007). Historically,\x20
| forest dynamics and patterns resulting from various fire regimes\x20
| largely influenced the susceptibility of stands to bark beetle\x20
| 18 M.J. Jenkins et al. / Forest Ecology and Management 254 (2008) 16–34 infestation. More recently, management practices including fire\x20
| suppression and exclusion, livestock grazing, and timber\x20
| harvesting have altered forest structure and composition (Arno,\x20
| 1980; Covington et al., 1997). The specific effect of altered\x20
| forest composition and structure on bark beetle epidemiology\x20
| will vary with tree species, the effectiveness of fire suppression,\x20
| historic fire regime and geographic parameters. One conse­
| quence of change in some forest types is that larger and more\x20
| contiguous landscapes have become simultaneously suscep­
| tible to bark beetle outbreaks due to the high percentage of old,\x20
| large diameter, and less vigorous host trees (Covington et al.,\x20
| 1997; Samman and Logan, 2000; Gallant et al., 2003). In\x20
| general, factors that increase susceptible tree numbers and\x20
| decrease heterogeneity increase bark beetle hazard (Amman,\x20
| 1977; Schmid and Frye, 1976; Rudinsky, 1966). After\x20
| reviewing extensive literature, Keane et al. (2002) concluded\x20
| that fire suppression programs have resulted in atypical fuel\x20
| accumulations, declines in forest health, greater risk to\x20
| firefighters and increased likelihood of insect and disease\x20
| epidemics. Sibold and others (2006), however, found little\x20
| evidence to support the assertion that fire suppression practices\x20
| have altered the historic range of variability in their study area\x20
| in Rocky Mountain National Park.\x20
| In the Intermountain West, Douglas-fir beetle (D. pseudot­
| sugae Hopkins), mountain pine beetle (D. ponderosae\x20
| Hopkins), and spruce beetle (D. rufipennis Kirby) are capable\x20
| of causing extensive mortality of host trees in relatively short\x20
| periods of time (Mielke, 1950; Massey and Wygant, 1954;\x20
| Amman, 1977; Schmid and Frye, 1977; Furniss and Orr, 1978;\x20
| McGregor and Cole, 1985; Amman et al., 1988b; Patterson,\x20
| 1991; Schmitz and Gibson, 1996; Holsten et al., 1999; Samman\x20
| and Logan, 2000; Dymerski et al., 2001). This mortality alters\x20
| the amount, composition and arrangement of living and dead\x20
| biomass in various fuel complexes (Schmid and Frye, 1977;\x20
| Cole and Amman, 1980; McGregor and Cole, 1985; Hadley and\x20
| Veblen, 1993; Hadley, 1994; Jenkins et al., 1998; Hopkins,\x20
| 1909; Kulakowski et al., 2003). Stands with a significant loss of\x20
| overstory trees may also experience increased solar input\x20
| resulting in higher temperatures and decreased relative\x20
| humidity desiccating fuels and lowering fuel moisture content\x20
| (Brown, 1975). It has long been assumed that such changes in\x20
| fuel complexes increase the potential for fire occurrence, and\x20
| fire intensity and severity (Brown, 1975; Lotan et al., 1985).\x20
| Policies outlined in the National Fire Plan and the Healthy\x20
| Forest Restoration Act of 2003 assume that bark beetle\x20
| epidemics increase fire hazard for extended periods. It is true\x20
| that any factor that alters the quantity and/or quality of the fuels\x20
| complex potentially affects fire hazard. In reality, little research\x20
| provides specific, quantitative descriptions of bark beetle-
| caused modifications to forest fuels over time. Romme et al.\x20
| (2006) suggested that few scientific studies support the\x20
| assertion that insect outbreaks set the stage for severe forest\x20
| fires.\x20
| The majority of research to date has focused on the\x20
| influences of the mountain pine beetle on fuels accumulation\x20
| and subsequent wildfires in lodgepole pine (Pinus contorta\x20
| Dougl. ex Loud) forests (Brown, 1975; Geiszler et al., 1980; Armour, 1982; Lotan et al., 1985; Romme et al., 1986; Lynch\x20
| et al., 2006). Some research has investigated the spatial and\x20
| temporal relationships of historic spruce beetle outbreaks and\x20
| fires in Colorado (Bebi et al., 2003; Kulakowski et al., 2003;\x20
| Bigler et al., 2005; Kulakowski and Veblen, 2007). Research to\x20
| examine relationships between Douglas-fir beetle-caused tree\x20
| mortality and mixed severity wildfires is very limited.\x20
| Literature reviewed by Parker et al. (2006) presents an\x20
| uncritical acceptance of the hypothesis that fire exclusion is\x20
| the main cause of bark beetle outbreaks. The literature they\x20
| cite supports the contention that outbreaks contribute to a\x20
| buildup of standing and down fuels increasing fire occurrence\x20
| and severity. More empirical research has been done to\x20
| compare the extent of historic bark beetle outbreaks mapped\x20
| using aerial detection surveys. Kulakowski and Veblen (2007),\x20
| for example, used aerial detection survey maps and other\x20
| spatially explicit forest disturbance data to model the effect of\x20
| prior disturbances, including spruce beetle outbreaks, on\x20
| severity of wildfire in Colorado subalpine forests. They found\x20
| no difference in fire severity among stands with current\x20
| outbreaks and endemic stands. Using similar data layers,\x20
| Lynch et al. (2006) found that the odds of burning increased in\x20
| mountain pine beetle-killed stands in one of two outbreaks\x20
| studied following the Yellowstone fires of 1988. No significant\x20
| association was detected in the second mountain pine beetle\x20
| outbreak. Their data set was limited, however, because it did\x20
| not consider the historic stand conditions, and the temporal\x20
| and spatial variability of the beetle-influenced fuels stratum.\x20
| Lynch et al. (2006) indicated that the accuracy of aerial\x20
| detection survey data was more than sufficient for their\x20
| analysis. Kulakowski and Veblen (2007) suggested, however,\x20
| that errors inherent in aerial detection surveys of bark beetle\x20
| activity limit conclusions regarding the influence of bark\x20
| beetle outbreaks on fire extent and severity. They indicated\x20
| that future studies could benefit from methods that incorporate\x20
| ground surveys to better measure the extent of current and past\x20
| outbreak events. These studies are further confounded by\x20
| consideration of factors such as geography, climate and\x20
| disturbance history which make isolation of the cause and\x20
| effect relationship of fire/fuels/beetle factors difficult. Addi­
| tionally, they do not consider other important factors that\x20
| affect the spatial distribution of burned and unburned areas\x20
| such as real time fire weather.\x20
| Few studies for any species consist of fuels data collected in\x20
| actual outbreak areas or fires in bark beetle-affected fuels. In\x20
| this paper, we describe fire regimes in Intermountain Douglas-
| fir (Pseudotsuga menziesii var. glauca (Beissn.) Franco),\x20
| lodgepole pine, and Engelmann spruce (Picea engelmannii\x20
| Parry ex Engelm.)–subalpine fir (Abies lasiocarpa (Hook.)\x20
| Nutt.) forests and their associated bark beetle species. We use\x20
| actual fuels and stand data to describe changes in the fuels\x20
| complex that occur in bark beetle-affected stands in all three\x20
| species. We then use the fuels data to develop and describe\x20
| custom fuel models that predict fire behavior in endemic,\x20
| epidemic and post-epidemic stands. We compare and contrast\x20
| our data with existing research relating bark beetle-caused tree\x20
| mortality to fuels and potential changes in fire behavior. Finally,\x20
